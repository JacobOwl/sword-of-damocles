# Carthage's chronicle: the card (bet B6, "The other shore")

kind: history, for the facts below; the chronicle's voice and every sentence's wording are invention. The facts behind `data/carthage-chronicle.json`. Every quote was opened in the saved texts in Jacob's Phoenician-Punic World repo (read-only) and is checked again by `scripts/verify-cast-quotes.mjs`. Translations: the Loeb Diodorus (Oldfather) and Rackham's Aristotle, unless marked "the game's own rendering".

## The problem this card is honest about

No Carthaginian history of these years survives. Everything we know of Carthage in 405 to 395 BC comes from Greek writers, mostly Diodorus (writing about 60 to 30 BC, from earlier Greek historians such as Ephorus and Timaeus). So "Carthage's side" here is a reconstruction: the events of your game, told with the institutions and customs that Greek writers and Carthaginian inscriptions attest. Where a sentence follows a Greek passage, the game's footnote says so. Diodorus' own reading of Carthage's defeat (the gods punishing impiety, Fortune humbling the proud, 14.76.1) is a Greek moralist's frame; the game borrows it because it is the only frame the sources give, not because it is known to be how Carthaginians saw it.

## Naming the year: the suffetes

- **The custom.** A Carthaginian temple tariff found at Marseille (third century BC) is dated "in the time of [the magistrates Ḥaloṣ]ba'al the Suffes, son of Bodtinnīt, (grand)son of Bod['esmūn, and Ḥaloṣba'al] / the Suffes, son of Bodesmūn" (line 1 to 2, as translated in the saved Wikipedia article on the Marseille Tariff, `sources/gods-dead/plain/wiki-Marseille_Tariff.txt`). The article: the two suffetes "apparently were the eponymous magistrates of the city in the year the inscription was made", "comparable with the two eponymous consuls in Rome". That is a modern summary (it cites Krahmalkov 2000 and Van den Branden 1965), not a primary source I have read.
- **Later evidence for the office.** Livy, for 193 BC: "when the suffetes took their seats the next morning to administer justice" (34.61.14, Roberts, `sources/livy/hannibal-exile-peace-years.roberts.txt`).
- **The gap.** Both are about two centuries after this decade. Whether Carthage named its years by two suffetes as early as 405 is **not checked**: I have not opened a source on when the office took this form. The game uses it as the chronicle's frame and says so on the page.
- **Who.** The men named are men of Carthage's great houses in your game (the census), the first-named living ones who have held the office least. All invention.

## The council and the people

- Aristotle (about 340 to 330 BC): "The reference of some matters and not of others to the popular assembly rests with the kings in consultation with the Elders in case they agree unanimously, but failing that, these matters also lie with the people" (*Politics* 1273a). The chronicle's "the kings and the Elders could not agree, so the question of war went before the people" tells the game's own vote this way (the game logs when the people's assembly decided).
- In 397 the tyrant's letter: "When it had been read in the council and subsequently before the people, it came about that the Carthaginians were not a little distressed at the thought of war; for the plague had killed great numbers of them, and they were also totally unprepared" (Diodorus 14.47.2).
- Hiring abroad: Carthage "dispatched members of the senate with large sums of money to recruit mercenaries in Europe" (14.47.3).

## The general, "king by law"

- Diodorus 14.54.5, Loeb: "lawfully according Himilcon sovereign power". The Greek is "Ἰμίλκωνα βασιλέα κατὰ νόμον καταστήσαντες", literally "making Himilcon king according to law" (`sources/gods-dead/diodorus.greek.txt`). Greek writers call some Carthaginian leaders "kings"; what Punic title stands behind the word is a modern debate this card does not settle.
- The chronicle says "made king by law, and gave the whole war into his hands" when the council gives a general sole command.

## Tribute, levies and Libya

- The peace of 405: Gela, Camarina and the rest "may dwell in their cities, which shall be unfortified, but shall pay tribute to the Carthaginians" (13.114.1).
- The army of 396: "they gathered armaments from all Libya as well as from Iberia" (14.54.5).
- The plague after the peace: "the pestilence continued to rage no less in Libya also and great numbers both of the Carthaginians themselves and of their allies were struck down" (13.114.2); by 398 it "had destroyed the larger part of the inhabitants of Libya" (14.45.3).
- The Libyan revolt after 396: "οἱ σύμμαχοι καὶ πάλαι μὲν μισοῦντες τὸ βάρος τῆς τῶν Καρχηδονίων ἡγεμονίας" (14.77.1, Greek only in the saved texts). The game's own rendering: "the allies, who had long hated the weight of the Carthaginians' rule". The English Loeb of 14.77.1 to 14.77.3 is **not saved**; check the rendering when it is (already asked for in `docs/later.md` by an earlier thread).

## The gods

- Diodorus calls Carthage's god by a Greek name: in 406 at Acragas Himilcar "supplicated the gods after the custom of his people by sacrificing a young boy to Cronus and a multitude of cattle to Poseidon" (13.86.3).
- The Punic names are from inscriptions. The saved Wikipedia article on Punic religion (`sources/gods-dead/wiki-Punic_religion.txt`, citing Xella 2019 and Clifford 1990): at Carthage the divine couple "appears to have consisted of the god Baal Hammon and the goddess Tanit", and from the fifth century BC Tanit "bears the title 'Face of Baal' (pene Baal)". Modern summary, not a primary source. The one chronicle sentence that names them (the council's offerings) is invention built on it.
- Defeat as the gods' anger: "The Carthaginians, against whom the gods were clearly fighting, at first gathered in small groups and in great confusion and besought the deity to put an end to its wrath" and "they voted by every means to propitiate the gods who had been sinned against" (14.77.4).
- The Greek goddesses taken in: "they appointed their most renowned citizens to be priests of these goddesses, and consecrating statues of them with all solemnity, they conducted their rites, following the ritual used by the Greeks" (14.77.5).
- The sacrilege that began it: "Himilcon seized the suburb of Achradinê; and he also plundered the temples of both Demeter and Corê" (14.63.1).

## The war of 397 to 396, as the chronicle uses it

- Plague in the camp: "the plague first attacked the Libyans" (14.71.1).
- The fleet in the Great Harbour: "Himilcon decked his ships with the spoils taken from the enemy and put in at the great harbour of the Syracusans" (14.62.2).
- The flight: Himilcon "manned forty triremes during the night with the citizens of Carthage and began his flight, abandoning all the rest of his army" (14.75.4).
- Himilcon's end: "he went about the temples of the city in the cheapest clothing, charging himself with impiety and offering acknowledged retribution to heaven for his sins against the gods. In the end he passed sentence of death upon himself and starved himself to death" (14.76.4).
- The Sicels: "The Siceli, who had hated Dionysius from of old and now had an opportunity to revolt, went over in a body" (14.58.1).
- Motya's fall (seen from Carthage when you sack a Punic town): the Greeks "slew everyone they encountered" (14.53.1), and Dionysius "also sold as booty the Motyans who survived" (14.53.4).
- The Carthaginians in Syracuse in 398: "the Syracusans, with the permission of Dionysius, seized as plunder the property of the Phoenicians; for no small number of Carthaginians had their homes in Syracuse and rich possessions" (14.46.1).

## "What the Greek histories say" notes

Each year's heading can carry one line of what Diodorus reports Carthage doing that year (405, 398, 397, 396 have one; the others say the Greek histories are silent). These are history, shown beside the game's invented year, not part of it. Diodorus' years run from midsummer and the game's from autumn, so read each as "about" (see `docs/research/calendar.md`).

## What is invention

The voice; every sentence's wording; which log lines Carthage would record and which it would not hear of; the suffetes' names; the closing verdicts; the sentence naming Baal Hammon and Tanit. The chronicle never adds a fact the game's log does not hold: it can only retell what happened in your game.
