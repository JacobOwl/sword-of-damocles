The game numbers on each person (traits, ambition, opinion, rival type, starting office) are **invention** and are listed as such in the data. This card holds only what the texts say.

**Dates.** Diodorus counts by archon years, so every date here is a range. "About 404" means 404 or 403. Polyaenus and Plutarch often give no date at all, and the card says so.

## The ruler's family

**Dionysius.** "Dionysius, the son of Hermocrates" (Diodorus 13.91.3). This is a different Hermocrates from the general whose daughter he married. He rose "from a scribe and ordinary private citizen" (13.96.4). Polyaenus says he "acted as secretary to their generals" (5.2.2).

**Leptines, his brother.** By about 397, "Leptines his admiral" (14.48.4). At Catana he attacked "in no cowardly fashion, but without prudence" and was "driven off and compelled to flee to the open sea" (14.60.2, 14.60.4). After our period (about 390), he made peace between the Italian Greeks and the Lucanians "to his own advantage, but without any profit to Dionysius", so Dionysius "relieved Leptines of his command and appointed Thearides, his other brother, commander of the fleet" (14.102.3). Leptines had "two daughters", and gave one to Philistus "without so much as telling" Dionysius, who then "banished Philistus from Sicily" (Plutarch, *Dion* 11). Plutarch gives no date for that marriage. This corrects the older card `dionysius-405-403.md`, which said the marriage was not found.

**Thearides, his other brother.** Named in 14.102.3 (above), leads 30 ships to the Lipari islands (14.103.2 to 14.103.3) and leads the embassy to Olympia (14.109.2). All of this is after 395. Plutarch says Dionysius' daughter Arete married "his brother Thearides" (*Dion* 6). No text gives Thearides a role between 405 and 395.

**Theste, his sister, wife of Polyxenus.** Plutarch: "the elder tyrant also, as it would appear, had a sister, Theste, whose husband, Polyxenus, had become his enemy. When, therefore, Polyxenus was moved by fear to run away and go into exile from Sicily, the tyrant sent for his sister and upbraided her" (*Dion* 21). Her answer: "it would have been well for me to be called the wife of Polyxenus the exile, rather than the sister of Dionysius the tyrant." No date is given for the flight.

**Heloris, friend or adoptive father.** "Heloris, one of his friends, or as some say, his adopted father" said "Tyranny is a fair winding-sheet" (14.8.5, about 404). Later he "had been banished by Dionysius" (14.103.5). By about 394 he is attacking Messene (14.87.2) and is "their general Heloris" at Rhegium (14.90.5). In about 389 Croton "chose as general Heloris the Syracusan", trusted "because of his hatred, to lead a war against the tyrant" (14.103.5). He dies at the Eleporus (14.104.3). The date of his banishment is not given; it falls between about 404 and about 394.

**Doris of Locri.** About 398, the Locrians approve the marriage and Dionysius "sued for the hand of Doris, the daughter of Xenetus, who at that time was their most esteemed citizen" (14.44.6). She is brought on "a quinquereme, the first one he had built" (14.44.7). Plutarch: "Doris had the good fortune to become a mother first, and by presenting Dionysius with his eldest son she atoned for her foreign birth" (*Dion* 3). Dionysius "accused the mother of his Locrian wife of giving Aristomache drugs to prevent conception, and put her to death" (*Dion* 3; no date).

**Aristomache, daughter of Hipparinus.** "The most notable maiden among them, Aristomachê" (14.44.8). Plutarch: "Aristomache, daughter of Hipparinus" (*Dion* 3), married on the same day as Doris. She "was for a long time a barren wife" (*Dion* 3).

**The first wife, daughter of Hermocrates.** Dead before the game starts. Married 406/405 (13.96.3). The sources disagree on her death. Diodorus: "slain at the time the cavalry revolted" (14.44.5). Plutarch: "outrageously abused ... and in consequence put an end to her own life" (*Dion* 3). Show both.

**Doricus.** About 404, at Herbessus: "The man appointed by Dionysius to command the men ... whose name was Doricus" is killed by the citizen soldiers (14.7.6 to 14.7.7).

**Andron.** Polyaenus, no date: "When Dionysius left on a foreign expedition, he left Andron in charge of the fortress and the treasury. Hermocrates advised Andron to seize both of these". Andron confessed when asked, and Dionysius "ordered him to be executed, because he had not revealed the proposal" at once (5.2.4). Polyaenus is late (2nd century AD) and anecdotal; treat the story as a story.

## The rich families

**Philistus.** In 406/405 he paid Dionysius' fine: "Philistus, who later composed his History, a man of great wealth, paid the fine" and offered to pay it all day (13.91.4). About 404, against flight: a tyrant should leave only "dragged by the leg" (14.8.5). Plutarch: he "at the outset had most zealously assisted in establishing the tyranny, and for a long time was commander of the garrison that guarded the citadel" (*Dion* 11). Also *Dion* 11: "There was a story, too, that he was very intimate with the mother of the elder Dionysius". Plutarch calls this a story; so should the game.

**Hipparinus.** Aristotle: "Hipparinus put forward Dionysius at Syracuse" (*Politics* 1306a). Plutarch: "a leading man in Syracuse, and had been a colleague of Dionysius when he was first chosen general with full powers for the war" (*Dion* 3). Father of Aristomache and of Dion ("Dion was a brother of Aristomache", *Dion* 4).

**Dion, son of Hipparinus.** A boy in these years. Plato, arriving "about forty years old" (about 388, a modern date, not checked here), says Dion was then "of the age which Hipparinus has now reached" (*Letter* 7.324), which does not fix a birth year. Kept as a name for family ties only.

**Polyxenus, brother-in-law.** Dionysius "gave his sister in marriage to Polyxenus, the brother of Hermocrates' wife" (13.96.3), "to draw a distinguished house into relationship with him". About 404 he advised flight: "to use his swiftest horse and ride off" (14.8.5). In 397/396 he is sent "as ambassador both to the Greeks in Italy and to the Lacedaemonians, as well as the Corinthians" (14.62.1) and returns "bringing thirty warships from his allies, with Pharacidas the Lacedaemonian as admiral" (14.63.4). Later he flees into exile (Plutarch, *Dion* 21, no date).
- **Disagreement.** Polyaenus says Dionysius imprisoned "Hermocrates, who had married his sister", then "to oblige her, he banished him to the Peloponnese" (5.2.4). Diodorus and Plutarch name Polyxenus as the sister's husband. These may be two sisters, or one story told two ways. The texts don't settle it.

**Theodorus.** About 396, in the assembly: "Theodorus, a Syracusan, who was held in high esteem among the cavalry and was considered a man of action, made bold to speak" (14.64.5). His demand: "if he is willing to lay down his office of his own accord, let us allow him to leave the city with his possessions" (14.69.4). Diodorus gives him a long speech (14.65 to 14.69); it is Diodorus' composition, not a transcript. The Spartan Pharacidas blocks the move (14.70.1 to 14.70.2). Nothing is said of Theodorus afterwards.

**Daphnaeus and Demarchus.** Dead before the game starts. Daphnaeus was the general elected for Acragas (13.86.4). Dionysius "had his most influential opponents, Daphnaeus and Demarchus, put to death" (13.96.3).

**The leader of the exiles at Aetna.** A role, not a name: no text names him. The surviving cavalry "fled from the city and occupied Aetnê" (13.113.3). About 404, "more than seven thousand of them were gathered with the cavalry at Aetnê" (14.9.5); some refuse terms and pray to see him get the same favour as the dead (14.9.7). Dionysius later "won the fortress, the exiles there being no match for an army of such size" (14.14.2).

## The common citizens

**Nicoteles the Corinthian.** About 404, the Spartan Aristus "slew Nicoteles the Corinthian, a leader of the Syracusans" (14.10.3). That is all we have.

**The assembly and the people.** The assembly made Dionysius general and then cried "to appoint him general with supreme power" (13.94.5). At Leontini he persuaded the people "to give him a guard of six hundred soldiers" (13.95.5). He "distributed the dwellings among the common people, except those on the island" (14.7.5). Then, "sending the Syracusans out to harvest their crops, entered their homes and carried off the arms of them all" (14.10.4).

## The mercenary guard

**Aristotle the Spartan.** About 396: "Dionysius, seeing that the mercenaries were most hostile to him and fearing that they might depose him, first of all arrested Aristotle, their commander" (14.78.1). He says he is sending Aristotle "to Lacedaemon to face trial" and gives the mercenaries, "about ten thousand", the city and land of Leontini "in lieu of their pay" (14.78.2). No text says when Aristotle took command.

**Archylus of Thurii.** About 397, at Motya, leads "the élite troops" (14.52.5); rewarded "with one hundred minas" for being "the first to mount the wall" (14.53.4).

**Biton of Syracuse.** About 397: "guards in the city whom he put under the command of Biton of Syracuse" at Motya (14.53.5).

**The houses on the Island.** Dionysius gave the Island's houses "to his friends and the mercenaries" (14.7.5). The guard's later demands for land have this behind them.

**The captain of the guard in 405.** Not in any saved text. Diodorus says Dionysius made "changes also in the military posts, conferring their commands upon his most faithful followers" (13.96.1), but names nobody. Any captain in 405 is invention.

## Outside powers (pressure only in Phase 1)

**Carthage.** Himilcon (Loeb also spells him Himilcar at 13.114.1) makes the 405 peace (13.114.1), leads the 397/396 war and flees by night after paying "three hundred talents" (14.75.4). Magon is his admiral in 396 (14.59.1) and commands "no less than eighty thousand" in about 393/392 (14.95.1), ending in a peace that gives Dionysius the Siceli and Tauromenium (14.96.4). Phase 1 keeps Carthage as a clock, so these two appear only in chronicle text and the clock's events.

**Sparta.** Aristus, sent about 404 "ostensibly pretending that they would overthrow the government, but in truth with intent to increase the power of the tyranny" (14.10.2). Pharacidas, admiral of the allied fleet in 396, "on friendly terms with the tyrant" (14.70.2). Diodorus also mentions "Aretes the Lacedaemonian" who "had betrayed" the Syracusans "on a previous occasion" (14.70.3). Whether Aretes is Aristus under another spelling is not settled in these texts.

**Catana, Naxos and Leontini.** About 403: Arcesilaus: "Arcesilaüs, the general of the Catanians, had offered to betray the city" (14.15.1); "Procles, the commander of the Naxians" did the same (14.15.2). The Leontines were ordered to "hand over their city and enjoy citizenship in Syracuse" (14.15.4).
