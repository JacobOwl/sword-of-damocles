## Dates

The start is the winter after the battle of Himera. Give it as a range: 480 to 479 BC.

- Himera. Amendola calls it "the celebrated battle of 480" (Amendola, section 3). Diodorus ties it to the Greek war with Persia, but the two ancient authors tie it to different days (see Disagreements). The Diodorus chapters before 11.20 are not in the saved files, so the archon year of the battle is not in the saved texts.
- Gelon takes Syracuse. Amendola gives "the refoundation of Syracuse by Gelon in 485" (Amendola, abstract). Pausanias gives an earlier date (see Disagreements).
- Gelon's reign length. Diodorus: "Gelon reigned for seven years" (Diodorus 11.38.7). Aristotle: "Gelo after being tyrant for seven years ended his life in the eighth" (Aristotle, Politics 1315b). So the start sits inside his reign, roughly the middle to late part, and he dies about 478 (this is my arithmetic from 485 plus seven, not a quoted date).
- Diodorus dates Gelon's death by an Athenian archon year, Timosthenes (Diodorus 11.38.1). The saved texts give no BC number for that year.
- In the saved Diodorus the Himera chapters end with "the most notable events which took place in this year" (Diodorus 11.26.8). The game year is therefore one Diodorus year, with the battle, the peace and the assembly all inside it.

## The six power objects

1. Citadel (Ortygia, the Island, and Achradina)
   - Holder at the start: not in the saved texts for 480. What the texts give is the ruler's hold on the whole city: "over Syracuse he reigned, and all his care was for Syracuse" (Herodotus 7.156.1). Later, in 466, the two fortified quarters are the tyrant's base: "the Island, which was fortified" (Diodorus 11.67.8).
   - Proposal (invention): Gelon and his household hold the Island (Ortygia) and its walls. Achradina is held by the new citizens he enrolled. The wife's family estate at the Nine Towers is a second stronghold: "His body was buried on the estate of his wife in the Nine Towers" (Diodorus 11.38.4).
2. Treasury
   - Holder: not in the saved texts for 480. What we have are the sums. Carthage pays a war indemnity: "two thousand talents of silver" (Diodorus 11.26.2). The best of the booty goes to the temples: "The fairest part of the booty he put to one side" (Diodorus 11.25.1). A gift to Apollo: "making a golden tripod of sixteen talents value" (Diodorus 11.26.7).
   - Proposal (invention): the ruler holds the treasury. Temples hold a second, sacred store. The game starts with a large cash pile that drains fast to pay mercenaries and building.
3. Mint
   - Holder: the ruler's household, shown by coin names. "she struck a coin which was called from her a Damareteion" (Diodorus 11.26.3). The wife is Damaretê: "a gold crown to Damaretê, the wife of Gelon" (Diodorus 11.26.3).
   - Coin evidence: the American Numismatic Society catalogue has Syracuse silver tetradrachms dated by the museum to 480 to 475 BC, with "quadriga r., Nike flying r. to crown horses" (ANS MANTIS tetradrachm list) on one face and "Arethusa hd. r., four dolphins around" (ANS MANTIS tetradrachm list) on the other. The catalogue date ranges are the museum's, and they do not say who controlled the mint.
   - Proposal (invention): Gelon controls the mint. Damarete has a personal share. A bad silver supply is a choice, not an accident.
4. Fleet
   - Holder: Gelon, by his own boast. "the leader of an army many times greater than yours and more ships by far" (Herodotus 7.160.2). He offers "two hundred triremes" (Herodotus 7.158.4). The offer is not proof that he owned them.
   - Diodorus: "he was making ready to sail to Greece with a large force" (Diodorus 11.26.4). Diodorus gives that force no number.
   - Proposal (invention): the fleet is a standing core of 60 to 80 ships, cut to a number the game can show. Rowers are citizens from the poorer ranks, so a ruler who pays them wins their loyalty. This is a game number only.
5. Generalship
   - Holder: Gelon. "gained his success primarily by reason of his own skill as a general" (Diodorus 11.22.5). Hamilcar, the opposing general, is dead on the day of the battle: "slew him, and then set fire to the ships" (Diodorus 11.22.1).
   - Gelon's own condition for sending help to Greece: "I shall be general and leader of the Greeks against the foreigner" (Herodotus 7.158.5).
   - Proposal (invention): the generalship is a separate office the ruler holds in his own person. After Gelon, it can be given away to a brother.
6. Assembly
   - Holder: summoned by Gelon, not independent. "he called them to an assembly, issuing orders for each man to appear fully armed" (Diodorus 11.26.5). The test: he came "not only with no arms but not even wearing a tunic" (Diodorus 11.26.5). The vote that follows: "they united in acclaiming him with one voice Benefactor and Saviour and King" (Diodorus 11.26.6).
   - Proposal (invention): the assembly meets only when the ruler calls it. It can acclaim and it can refuse acclaim. It cannot make law on its own at this start.

## The factions

1. The old landowners (the gamoroi). Herodotus: "the so-called landed gentry of Syracuse, who had been driven into exile by the common people" (Herodotus 7.155.2). Gelon brought them home. Amendola reads Gelon's rise as help given to the ousted gamoroi against the demos (Amendola, section 2, paraphrase, no quote).
2. The common people (the demos). They gave in: "the Syracusan common people surrendered themselves and it to Gelon at his coming" (Herodotus 7.155.2). Gelon's view of them: "the common people to be exceedingly disagreeable to live with" (Herodotus 7.156.3).
3. The slave class tied to the old landowners. The same sentence in Herodotus names them: "and their slaves, the Cyllyrians" (Herodotus 7.155.2).
4. The newcomers brought in by force or by choice. The wealthy of Megara: "he took the wealthier of them" (Herodotus 7.156.2), and made them citizens: "brought them to Syracuse to be citizens there" (Herodotus 7.156.2). The commons of Megara: "sold them for slaves to be taken out of Sicily" (Herodotus 7.156.2).
5. The mercenaries made citizens. Diodorus mentions them only when he looks back from 466: "Gelon had enrolled as citizens more than ten thousand foreign mercenaries" (Diodorus 11.72.3). Whether it happened before or after Himera is disputed (see Disagreements). Aristotle's view of the type of ruler: "a king’s body-guard consists of citizens, a tyrant’s of foreign mercenaries" (Aristotle, Politics 1311a).
6. The ruler's family. Brothers: "he handed over the kingship to Hieron, his eldest brother" (Diodorus 11.38.3). Another brother, Polyzelus, is popular: "observing how popular his brother Polyzelus was among the Syracusans" (Diodorus 11.48.3). A third brother, Thrasybulus, appears later: "he left the kingdom to his brother Thrasybulus" (Diodorus 11.66.4). The wife is a power in her own right: "Damaretê at their request had contributed the greatest aid toward the conclusion of the peace" (Diodorus 11.26.3).
7. The allies who fought at Himera (Theron and the Acragantini). They get the prisoners: "many private citizens had five hundred captives in their homes" (Diodorus 11.25.2).

## Carthage's posture

Beaten, frightened, paying, and quiet for now.

- It lost the general and the army. Diodorus, in his own rhetoric: "not only did the general perish but also everyone who participated in the war was slain" (Diodorus 11.23.2).
- It feared invasion. "every night they kept vigil guarding the city" (Diodorus 11.24.3).
- It begged and paid. "he granted them peace, exacting of them the expense he had incurred for the war, two thousand talents of silver" (Diodorus 11.26.2).
- After the peace: "the Carthaginians having finally been humbled" (Diodorus 11.38.1). A poet prays for it to stay so: "the battle-shouts of the Carthaginians and Etruscans stay quietly at home" (Pindar, Pythian 1).
- Carthage still holds the west of the island: Hamilcar landed at "the harbour of Panormus" (Diodorus 11.20.2). Pausanias on Motya: "the city Motye, inhabited by Libyans and Phoenicians" (Pausanias 5.25.5).
- Carthage also keeps honouring its dead general: "monuments have been set up in all the colonists' cities" (Herodotus 7.167.2).
- Whether Carthage has a treaty text in force, and how long the peace is, is not in the saved texts beyond what is quoted. Diodorus adds a temple clause: "build two temples in which they should place copies of the treaty" (Diodorus 11.26.2).

## The island

- Syracuse: Gelon. See above.
- Gela: under Gelon's brother. "which he gave in charge to his brother Hiero" (Herodotus 7.156.1). Gela is also where Gelon's own family came from: "Gelon, a descendant of the ministering priest Telines" (Herodotus 7.154.1).
- Acragas: ruled by Theron, Gelon's ally and partner. "Theron, the ruler of the Acragantini" (Diodorus 11.20.5). It grows rich on the war: "the Acragantini, who embellished their city and countryside" (Diodorus 11.25.2).
- Himera: Theron holds it. The old tyrant was thrown out: "Terillus son of Crinippus, the tyrant of Himera" (Herodotus 7.165.1), "expelled from Himera by Theron son of Aenesidemus" (Herodotus 7.165.1).
- Rhegium and Zancle: ruled by Anaxilas, who sided with Carthage. "Anaxilaus son of Cretines" (Herodotus 7.165.1) is named as the main promoter. His hostage deal: "He had handed over his own children as hostages to Amilcas" (Herodotus 7.165.1). Diodorus spells him Anaxilas.
- Selinus: on the Carthaginian side, with a promise to send horse. "dispatches from the people of Selinus" (Diodorus 11.21.4).
- Camarina, the rest of Gela, Megara Hyblaea, Euboea: emptied or reduced into Syracuse. "bring all the people of Camarina to Syracuse and give them its citizenship" (Herodotus 7.156.2).
- The Sicel inland: not named in the saved texts for 480 beyond Diodorus's later list of Sicel cities (Diodorus 11.68.1, quoted in the 466 dossier). Not in the saved texts for this year.

## About 15 people

Real people (history):

1. Gelon, son of Deinomenes, tyrant, "King of the Syracusans" (Herodotus 7.161.1). Role: the default player. "Gelon, excelling as he did in skill as a general and in shrewdness" (Diodorus 11.21.3).
2. Damaretê, wife of Gelon, mint-holder by name. "Damaretê, the wife of Gelon" (Diodorus 11.26.3).
3. Hieron, eldest brother, ruler of Gela, later successor. "his brother Hiero" (Herodotus 7.156.1); "Hieron, his eldest brother" (Diodorus 11.38.3). Praised in verse as "commander of the Syracusans" (Bacchylides 5).
4. Polyzelus, brother, popular with the citizens. "his brother Polyzelus" (Diodorus 11.48.3). His role in 480 itself is not in the saved texts.
5. Thrasybulus, youngest brother in the sources, later ruler. "his brother Thrasybulus" (Diodorus 11.66.4). His role in 480 is not in the saved texts.
6. Theron, ruler of Acragas, ally. "Theron, the ruler of the Acragantini" (Diodorus 11.20.5).
7. Hamilcar, Carthaginian general, dead at the start. "they chose for general Hamilcar" (Diodorus 11.20.1). His fate has two stories (see Disagreements).
8. Terillus of Himera, exile who brought Carthage in. "Terillus son of Crinippus, the tyrant of Himera" (Herodotus 7.165.1).
9. Anaxilas (Anaxilaus) of Rhegium and Zancle. "Anaxilaus son of Cretines" (Herodotus 7.165.1).
10. Phormis of Maenalus, an Arcadian soldier who rose under Gelon: "He crossed to Sicily from Maenalus to serve Gelon the son of Deinomenes" (Pausanias 5.27.1). His rank is not in the saved texts; his later wealth is: "Distinguishing himself in the campaigns of Gelon and afterwards of his brother Hieron" (Pausanias 5.27.1).
11. Cadmus of Cos, Gelon's envoy with money to Delphi. "sent Cadmus son of Scythes, a man of Cos, to Delphi with three fifty-oared ships" (Herodotus 7.163.2).
12. Micythus, steward of Anaxilas and later his regent. "this Micythus, when Anaxilas was despot of Rhegium, became his slave and steward of his property" (Pausanias 5.26.4). That is Pausanias quoting Herodotus; Herodotus's own text on Micythus is not in the saved files.

Invented people (invention), with names taken from the Greek names in the saved texts. They are not the real men who bear those names:

13. Philocles of the old landowners, a leading gamoros who came home from Casmena. Why: the old families need a face, and Herodotus names the group only as "the so-called landed gentry of Syracuse" (Herodotus 7.155.2). The name is borrowed from the Athenian archon list in Diodorus (Diodorus 11.78.1).
14. Conon, an Arcadian cavalry captain of the new citizens. Why: the mercenaries made citizens need a spokesman. He is modelled on Phormis but is not Phormis. Name borrowed from Diodorus 11.74.1.
15. Euthippus, a freed Cyllyrian who now farms for a gamoros. Why: Herodotus names the Cyllyrians, but never a person. Name borrowed from Diodorus 11.75.1.
16. Mnesitheides, a rich man of Megara taken to Syracuse. Why: Herodotus says the wealthy of Megara were enrolled, and the player needs a face for that group. Name borrowed from Diodorus 11.81.1.
17. A Syracusan poet-priest of Demeter and Kore (name left open for the game). Why: Gelon "built noteworthy temples to Demeter and Corê out of the spoils" (Diodorus 11.26.7), so the cult needs a living voice.

## What the player starts as

Default: Gelon, who holds Syracuse. He holds the generalship, the Island, the mint, the fleet and the treasury at the high point of his rule. The game question is how to stay loved. He is described as "died in the esteem of his people" (Diodorus 11.23.3), and this is the standard the sword check will test.

Two other playable starts:

1. Hieron, ruler of Gela and eldest brother. Why: Gelon gave Gela to him (Herodotus 7.156.1) and the game can ask whether he waits for the crown or reaches for it. Gelon names him heir: "he handed over the kingship to Hieron, his eldest brother" (Diodorus 11.38.3). Hieron is later shown to distrust Polyzelus (Diodorus 11.48.3), and the game can recreate that.
2. Phormis, a mercenary captain of Arcadian origin who rises in Gelon's service. Why: the sources show a real foreign soldier who became rich and dedicated offerings at Olympia and Delphi (Pausanias 5.27.1). The player begins with a company, little land and no vote, and can try to be one of the more than ten thousand made citizens.

An optional third (invention): Philocles, the returned landowner, starting with land but no office.

## Stories this start can make

None of these is scripted. Each is a set of pressures the simulation should be able to produce.

1. The tyrant who must be loved. Gelon walks into the assembly with no weapon and is acclaimed. "stepping forward he rendered an account of his whole life" (Diodorus 11.26.5). The game gives a ruler a trust meter. If trust is high, a test like this is possible. If it is low, the same gesture is a death. (Sword check: warnings are visible before the blow.)
2. Brothers. Hieron suspects Polyzelus: "believing that he was waiting to seize the kingship" (Diodorus 11.48.3). Polyzelus runs: "Polyzelus took refuge with Theron" (Diodorus 11.48.5). The simulation should be able to produce a family split after the ruler dies, over a popular brother.
3. The new citizens. Gelon enrolled mercenaries as citizens (Diodorus 11.72.3), and Aristotle describes the later cost: "the Syracusans after the period of the tyrants conferred citizenship on their foreign troops and mercenaries and then faction set in" (Aristotle, Politics 1303b). The simulation should model pay, land and political voice for the new citizens as a loyalty pressure on whoever rules.
4. Plunder and captives. The spoils are shared out by the number each ally sent: "apportioning it in accordance with the number who had served with him" (Diodorus 11.25.1). Acragas ends up with the most. The game can let the ally grow stronger than the ruler.
5. The exiled enemies. Terillus and Anaxilas lost the contest, and Anaxilas is Carthage's friend (Herodotus 7.165.1). A story of revenge, a plot, or a marriage tie can come from them.

## Disagreements

1. Why Carthage came. Diodorus: "The Carthaginians, we recall, had agreed with the Persians to subdue the Greeks of Sicily at the same time" (Diodorus 11.20.1). Herodotus: Terillus brought Hamilcar, "partly through the prerogative of personal friendship" (Herodotus 7.165.1) and "mainly through the efforts of Anaxilaus son of Cretines" (Herodotus 7.165.1). Both readings are kept. The game need not choose.
2. Which day. Herodotus: "on the same day that the Greeks defeated the Persian at Salamis" (Herodotus 7.166.1). Diodorus: "on the same day that Leonidas and his soldiers were contesting against Xerxes at Thermopylae" (Diodorus 11.24.1). In the same chapters Diodorus also has Gelon stop his sailing plan only after word of Salamis arrives (Diodorus 11.26.5). The ancient sources do not agree on the day.
3. How Hamilcar died. Diodorus: cavalry killed him at the sacrifice, "slew him, and then set fire to the ships" (Diodorus 11.22.1). Herodotus (the Sicilian story): "he vanished from sight" (Herodotus 7.166.1). Herodotus (the Carthaginian story): "he cast himself into the fire where he was pouring libations on the sacrifice" (Herodotus 7.167.1).
4. Gelon as ruler. Diodorus: "Gelon had established a beneficent rule over the Sicilian Greeks" (Diodorus 11.38.1). Herodotus tells of the Megarian commons being "sold them for slaves to be taken out of Sicily" (Herodotus 7.156.2). Both can be true at once, and the game should let both happen.
5. How long Gelon ruled. Diodorus has him "grew old in the kingship" (Diodorus 11.23.3). The same author says "Gelon reigned for seven years" (Diodorus 11.38.7). Aristotle: seven years, dying in the eighth (Aristotle, Politics 1315b). Diodorus disagrees with himself on tone, not on number.
6. When Gelon took Syracuse. Pausanias: "the Gelon who was tyrant of Sicily took possession of Syracuse when Hybrilides was archon at Athens, in the second year of the seventy-second Olympiad" (Pausanias 6.9.5). Amendola's note calls that date "evidently mistaken: cf. Maddoli et al. 1999, 241" (Amendola, note 28), and he gives "the refoundation of Syracuse by Gelon in 485" (Amendola, abstract). Both are shown. This dossier uses 485 as a modern reading, not as a fact.
7. When the mercenaries were enfranchised. Amendola: the debate is "the refoundation of Syracuse in 485 or after the battle of Himera in 480" (Amendola, section 2). He finds the earlier date more convincing, but it is a scholar's argument, not a text.
8. How many mercenaries. Diodorus: "more than ten thousand foreign mercenaries" (Diodorus 11.72.3). Amendola's note on Bettalli: the share still alive later "sembra una percentuale stranamente assai elevata" (Amendola, note 43). That is, a modern scholar finds the survivor share suspiciously high. My plain-English gloss: it seems strangely high. The game should use ranges, not the round numbers.
9. The Himera battle numbers. Carthage: "an army of not less than three hundred thousand men" (Diodorus 11.20.2), and Herodotus has the same: "three hundred thousand Phoenicians, Libyans, Iberians, Ligyes, Elisyci, Sardinians, and Cyrnians" (Herodotus 7.165.1). Dead: "no less than one hundred and fifty thousand of them were slain" (Diodorus 11.22.4). Gelon's army: "not less than fifty thousand foot-soldiers" (Diodorus 11.21.1). These are huge, ancient and round. Treat them as ranges the game may scale down.

## Not found in the saved texts

- The archon year and the BC date of the battle of Himera, and of Gelon's death, in saved Diodorus (only the heading for his death, Timosthenes, is saved).
- Any list of Syracusan offices at the start: magistrates, councils, tribes, who counts as an old citizen.
- Who holds the citadel, treasury and fleet by name in 480.
- The ships, sailors and talents Gelon actually had. The only figure is his own offer to Greece, which is a boast.
- The treaty text with Carthage and the exact meaning of the temple clause.
- Damaretê's role beyond the coin and the peace.
- The rank of Phormis in 480.
- Where the Sicel peoples stand in 480.
- The size of the Syracusan citizen body, old or new, other than the more-than-ten-thousand mercenary figure.
- Whether the Nine Towers estate sat on the citizen land or outside it. Diodorus gives only a distance of two hundred stades (Diodorus 11.38.4).
- Anything on grain supply, land tenure or taxes, which the game would need for the people layer.
