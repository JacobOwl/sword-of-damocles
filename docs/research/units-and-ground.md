# Who fought, and on what ground (c. 409 to 262 BC)

kind: history. The facts behind `docs/council/2026-11-27-units-ground-navy.md`. Nothing here is built. Every quote was opened on 2026-11-27 in the saved texts of Jacob's Phoenician-Punic World repo (read-only): the Loeb Diodorus (Oldfather) under `sources/diodorus/english/`, Plutarch (Perrin) `sources/plutarch/lives-carthage.perrin.txt`, Polybius (Shuckburgh) `sources/polybius/book1.shuckburgh.txt`, Devereaux under `sources/acoup/`. Numbers are the ancient writers' and are often far too large; where two writers disagree, both are given.

## 1. What the armies were made of, 398 to 396

**Dionysius' army.** "He had eighty thousand infantry, well over three thousand cavalry, and a little less than two hundred warships, and he was accompanied by not less than five hundred merchantmen loaded with great numbers of engines of war" (14.47.7, 397, the march on Motya). A year later, defending: "thirty thousand infantry, more than three thousand cavalry, and one hundred and eighty ships of war" (14.58.2).

- **Many nations, each in its own arms.** "he distributed among them models of each kind, because he had gathered his mercenaries from many nations; for he was eager to have every one of his soldiers armed with the weapons of his people ... in battle all of his soldiers would fight to best effect in armour to which they were accustomed" (14.41.4-5).
- **The arms made.** "one hundred and forty thousand shields and a like number of daggers and helmets ... corselets ... more than fourteen thousand in number. These Dionysius expected to distribute to his cavalry and the commanders of the infantry, as well as to the mercenaries who were to form his bodyguard" (14.43.2-3). So most men were lightly armoured; the corselets went to horse, officers and the guard.
- **Engines.** "the catapult was invented at this time in Syracuse" (14.42.1, 399). At Motya "he advanced war engines of every kind against the walls ... while with the catapults he kept down the fighters on the" battlements (14.51.1). Engines are for sieges.
- **Levies by town.** "He received first the levy from Camarina, then those of Gela and Acragas; and after these he sent for the Himeraeans ... and after adding the men of Selinus" (14.47.6). Each town's men came as a body.
- **Citizens in companies.** "From the Syracusans he enrolled those who were fit for military service in companies and from the cities subject to him he summoned their able men. He also gathered mercenaries from Greece, and especially from the Lacedaemonians" (14.44.2).

**Carthage's army.** "they gathered armaments from all Libya as well as from Iberia, summoning some from their allies and in other cases hiring mercenaries. In the end they collected more than three hundred thousand infantry, four thousand cavalry in addition to chariots, which numbered four hundred ... These are the numbers stated by Ephorus" (14.54.5). **Disagreement:** "Timaeus, on the other hand, says that the troops transported from Libya did not exceed one hundred thousand and declares that an additional thirty thousand were enlisted in Sicily" (14.54.6). Treat every size as a range, and the chariots as Ephorus' figure only.

- **Who was in it.** Libyans (the plague "first attacked the Libyans", 14.71.1), Iberians (14.75.8-9), Campanians (13.44.1, 13.85.4), Sicels who joined in 396 (14.58.1), later Sardinians and Italians (14.95.1, about 393).
- **Also in their own arms.** In 393 Magon's soldiers "were all carefully supplied with equipment to which they were accustomed" (14.95.1).
- **A modern view of how it held together.** Carthage's generals worked as "a sort of personalistic interface with these communities" (Devereaux, ACOUP, "Raising Carthaginian Armies" part 3, of the third century; a modern reading, not a source). In battle Hannibal's Africans were "often deployed in reserved positions" (part 2, of 218 to 216).

## 2. Horsemen acted as one political body

**Inference, not stated:** no saved text says the horsemen *were* the rich families. What Diodorus shows is the cavalry acting as one body against the tyrant (13.112.3, 13.113.2-3, 14.7.7) and being discharged as "of no use in the siege" (14.9.1). The game rule "the horsemen read the rich families' mood" is an inference from that (historian seat, 2026-11-27).

- At Gela in 405 the Syracusan horsemen turned on Dionysius: they "kept watch in the hope that they might be able to slay the tyrant along the road; but when they saw that the mercenaries were not deserting him, they rode off with one accord to Syracuse" (13.112.3). His wife "had been slain at the time the cavalry revolted" (14.44.5). The survivors "fled from the city and occ[upied]" Aetna (13.113.3).
- A cavalry man speaks for liberty against him in 396: "Theodorus, a Syracusan, who was held in high esteem among the cavalry" (14.64.5).
- What horse did in the field here: screening, foraging and cutting off foragers more than charging. Daphnaeus at Acragas in 406, "by covering the roads with his cavalry he seized such as were foraging" (13.88.1). At Gela in 405, "with his light armed troops he engaged the enemy and did not allow them the forage over the countryside, while with his cavalry and ships he attempted to deprive the Carthaginians" of supplies (13.109.3).

## 3. Contingents that left, broke, or changed sides (Jacob's question)

All Diodorus unless marked. These are the history behind "5,000 from Gela who may backstab us".

1. **Allies go home when the campaign drags (406).** At Acragas a Spartan officer was said to be bribed: "Dexippus the Lacedaemonian was corrupted by a bribe of fifteen talents ... Consequently the generals, offering as their excuse that the time agreed upon for the campaign had elapsed, led their troops off to the Strait" (13.88.7). Diodorus gives the bribe as "the story is told".
2. **Mercenaries who feel underpaid switch sides (409).** After Himera the Campanians "bitterly complained to the Carthaginians that, though they had been the ones chiefly responsible for the Carthaginian successes, the rewards they had received were not a fair return" (13.62.5); by 406 Campanians were fighting for Acragas (13.85.4).
3. **The hungry mercenaries mutiny (406).** In Carthage's camp at Acragas "the Campanians together with the other mercenaries, almost in a body, forced their way to the tent" of the general (13.88.2).
4. **After a defeat, the Italian Greeks walk home (405).** "Now the Italian Greeks, deserting Dionysius, made their way home through the interior of the island" (13.112.3), and the horsemen rode off to overthrow him (above).
5. **Different peoples fought differently at Gela (405).** "The Iberians and Campanians, who were serving in the army of the Carthaginians, pressing hard upon the Italian Greeks, slew more than a thousand of them" (13.110.5); on the other side "the Sicilian Greeks, who had engaged the Libyans who opposed them, slew great numbers of them ... but when the Iberians and Campanians and, besides, the Carthaginians came up to the aid of the Libyans, they withdrew" (13.110.6).
6. **A whole people changes sides (396).** "The Siceli, who had hated Dionysius from of old and now had an opportunity to revolt, went over in a body, with the exception of the people of Assorus, to the Carthaginians" (14.58.1).
7. **Held by hostages (396).** Himilcon offered the Campanians of Aetna land and spoils to change sides, "But since the Campanians had given hostages to Dionysius and had sent their choicest troops to Syracuse, they were compelled to maintain the alliance with Dionysius, although they would gladly have joined the Carthaginians" (14.61.6).
8. **Angry allies leave when you won't fight (396).** "The majority of the Sicilian Greeks, being angered at his unwillingness to encounter the enemy, deserted Dionysius, some of them departing to their own countries and others to fortresses in the neighbourhood" (14.61.3). Already used: the `armySupply` drift-home rule.
9. **The men you distrust, sent in first (396).** "These mercenaries were the most hostile, beyond all others, to Dionysius ... Dionysius had issued orders to the cavalry that as soon as they came to blows with the enemy they should flee and leave the mercenaries in the lurch" (14.72.2-3). Already used: a battle-plate stratagem.
10. **Carthage's levies run home first (396).** After Himilcon slipped away, "the Siceli, who were serving in the army of the Carthaginians, forestalling the Syracusans, fled through the interior and, almost to a man, made their way in safety to their native homes" (14.75.6); the Iberians alone held together and were taken into Dionysius' pay: "Some Iberians alone massed together with their arms ... Dionysius made peace with the Iberians and enrolled them in his mercenaries" (14.75.8-9).
11. **Your own citizens leave over strategy (about 393).** At the Chrysas, "The Syracusans were for deciding the issue by battle as soon as possible, but Dionysius opposed them, saying that time and want would ruin the barbarians without fighting. Provoked to anger at this the Syracusans deserted him" (14.96.2).
12. **A wing breaks when its commander falls (Diodorus dates it to the archonship of Phanostratus, 15.15.1, about 383/2; some modern accounts put it later, about 375: a range, unverified).** At Cronium, "Leptines, who was stationed on one wing ... ended his life in a blaze of glory ... At his fall the Phoenicians were emboldened and pressed so hard upon their opponents that they put them to flight" (15.17.1); "when the death of Leptines became known and the other wing was crushed, his men were dismayed and took to flight" (15.17.2). A pattern for later; not a 405 to 395 event.

## 4. Ground and the general who knows it

- **Locals know the land (about 393).** Agyris' men, "being familiar with the territory, held the advantage in laying ambushes and were continually cutting off the enemy's supplies" (14.96.1); Magon was "encamped in hostile territory and was ever more and more in want of supplies" (same).
- **A river in front (405).** At Gela the horsemen were ordered "as soon as they saw the infantry advancing, they should cross the river and overrun the plain" (13.109.5).
- **Marsh and camps.** "the terrain was marshy" (14.70.5), and the camp lay "in a narrow pla[ce]" (14.70.6). Already in the game as plague.
- **Caught crossing a river, with weather (339, a later pattern).** Plutarch: "the river exhaled a thick mist which at first hid the plain" (Timoleon 27). Diodorus: "As all sought the river together — horse and foot intermingled, while the chariots added to the confusion — some perished helplessly trodden under foot" (16.80.2); "Most of the chariots were destroyed in the battle but two hundred were taken" (16.80.5). Timoleon "advanced down from a line of little hills to the river" (16.79.5).

## 5. Elephants: later, and not Carthage's first

- In Sicily the first elephants in the saved texts are **Pyrrhus'**, about 278: "He embarked his men, his elephan[ts]" (Diodorus 22.8.2); "thirty thousand infantry, fifteen hundred cavalry and his elephants" (22.10.2). BC year from the usual dating; the saved text gives no BC year here (unverified).
- **Carthage's** elephants in Sicily first appear at Acragas in the First Punic War, 262: "he accordingly collected his elephants, of which he had about fifty" (Polybius 1.19). That is two years after the game's last year (264).
- How they were beaten (250, Panormus): Caecilius sent "his light-armed troops to harass them" and "placed some of his light troops to line the wall and moat, with instructions that if the elephants came within range they should pour" missiles on them (Polybius 1.40). A pattern only.

So: no elephants in 405 to 395. Pyrrhus' elephants fit the 289 campaign, as a foreign army; Carthage's do not fit any campaign inside 480 to 264 by the saved texts.

## Added by the council's historian seat (2026-11-27)

Opened by the seat in the saved texts; see `docs/council/2026-11-27-units/historian.md` for the full statement.

- **Fighting before their own walls.** At Himera the townsmen had "as spectators on the walls parents and children" (13.60.4). At Gela the townsmen beat foragers "because of their knowledge of the land" (13.108.7), but in the battle gave aid "over only a short space ... since they were afraid to abandon the guarding of the walls" (13.110.4).
- **Caught crossing, not crossing itself.** Crimisus: "the phalanx of the enemy was sundered by the river" (Plutarch, Timoleon 27); "ten thousand of the enemy had already crossed" (16.79.5). The Himeras, 311: "for a long time neither dared to cross the river in force" (19.108.3). Counter-case, 406: the Syracusans "had already crossed the Himera River" and still won (13.87.1).
- **Pursuit has a risk.** Himera, 409: Hannibal "caught the Himeraeans in disorder as they were pushing the pursuit" (13.60.6); at Acragas the general held back, "he remembered also how the Himeraeans had been utterly destroyed" (13.87.2). Ships cover a retreat: "the crews of the ships held back the pursuers with showers of arrows" (13.110.5).
- **A hill without water.** Elleporus, 389: "a hill, which was strong enough to withstand a siege but had no water" (14.105.1-3); Dionysius "let the prisoners go ... without ransom" (14.105.4). Cabala: "a hill which was fortified but altogether without water" (15.15.3).
- **A false report sends men home.** "the Leontines and Campanians who were guarding Epipolae ... in consequence of a false report which Dion sent to them that he would attack their cities first, deserted Timocrates and went off to assist their own peoples" (Plutarch, Dion 27).
- **Mercenaries and pay.** In Carthage's camp at Acragas, unfed mercenaries "threatened to go over to the enemy" (13.88.2); paid "in lieu of their pay" with Leontini (14.78.2-3); Entella seized by Campanians who "fell upon them by night" (14.9.9).
- **Carthage's citizens.** The "Sacred Battalion, twenty-five hundred ... distinguished for valour and reputation as well as for wealth" (16.80.4); afterwards "They voted not to risk the lives of citizens in the future but to enlist foreign mercenaries" (16.81.3-4). Plutarch gives 3,000 Carthaginians among 10,000 dead (Timoleon 28). Devereaux: Carthaginian citizen armies "are actually very common everywhere ... in the fifth and fourth century" (part 1, modern).
- **Defeat spreads.** After Tauromenium, Acragas and Messene "renounced their alliance with the tyrant" (14.88.5); after the Himeras, nearby towns "went over" (19.110.3-4).
- **Corrections to the arms.** Campanians also served as horse ("purchased horses for them all", 13.44.2; "twelve hundred cavalry", 14.9.2); calling Sicels light troops is invention. The one chariot action described is a screen against horse on open ground (Timoleon 27); "two hundred chariots" were lost at sea in 396 (14.55.3). Carthage had engines too: towers that "far excelled the walls in height" (13.55.7). Horse is never shown breaking infantry head-on in these years; it screens, forages, carries news (13.54.3) and pursues.
- **Distrusted men sent first, a second case.** Hiero "thrust [the mercenaries] to the front and allowed them to be completely cut to pieces" (Polybius 1.9).
