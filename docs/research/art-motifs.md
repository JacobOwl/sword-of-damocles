# Motifs for the art, with sources

kind: history. This card backs the two `kind: history` records in `data/flavor.json` (the title epigraph and the coin legend) and the sourced motifs in `docs/art-direction.md`. Everything drawn in the game is invention: no source shows these scenes or objects as the game draws them.

Both passages were searched for and found in the saved texts in Jacob's Phoenician-Punic World repo (read-only) on 2026-11-22.

## The title epigraph: Heloris

Diodorus 14.8.5 (Loeb, `sources/diodorus/english/book14-1-18-97-117.loeb.txt`), during the siege of Dionysius in the Island:

> Now Heloris, one of his friends, or as some say, his adopted father, declared to him, "Tyranny is a fair winding-sheet"

Undated in the game's terms; Diodorus puts it in his year of about 404 BC (a range: 404 or 403). Heloris is in the cast.

## The city's coin: ΣΥΡΑΚΟΣΙΩΝ, Arethusa and the dolphins

American Numismatic Society, record 2015.1.1, a silver tetradrachm of Syracuse dated "405 BCE - 400 BCE" (saved listing: `sources/sicily-places/ans-mantis-syracuse-tetradrachm-all.tsv`, scraped from numismatics.org on 2026-11-20):

> ΣYPAKOΣIΩ[N] in exergue: Charioteer, holding kentron in extended right hand and reins in left, driving fast quadriga left; above, Nike flying right, crowning charioteer with wreath held in both hands

> Head of Arethousa left, wearing wreath of grain ears, triple-pendant earring, and pearl necklace; four swimming dolphins around.

Used for: the legend ΣΥΡΑΚΟΣΙΩΝ ("of the Syracusans") on the title coin, the dolphin on the mint icon, the grain ear for the citizens. The ANS listing gives a date range; other Arethusa tetradrachms in the same file are dated more widely (413 to 357 BC).

## The sword itself

The Damocles story is in Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 5.61 to 5.62, which is **not saved**, and two saved modern pages disagree about which Dionysius it belongs to (see `events-405-395.md`). The title scene (a couch, a cup, a sword on a hair) is the game's title drawn, not a claim about Dionysius I.

## Carthage's coin: the horse and the palm

Checked 2026-11-27 in `sources/coinage/wikipedia-carthaginian-coinage.txt` (Phoenician-Punic World repo, a saved Wikipedia page, so a modern summary, not an ancient source):

> "The first attested Carthaginian coinage seems to have been minted in 410 or 409 BC, to pay for the massive Carthaginian military intervention in Sicily that led to the Second Sicilian War (410-404 BC) and it continued through until the end of the Third Sicilian War (398-393 BC)."

> "The obverse of these earliest coins bears the front half of a horse facing right, with a Punic language legend reading QRTḤDŠT (𐤒𐤓𐤕𐤇𐤃𐤔𐤕, 'Carthage'). The reverse depicts a date palm tree, with the inscription MḤNT (𐤌𐤇𐤍𐤕, 'the encampment')."

Dates: the page gives about 410 to 390 BC for this "Series I" silver, ending by the early 380s. **Disagreement shown, not decided:** what the horse and palm meant is disputed on the same page (the horse as Baal Hammon, as Justin's foundation omen, or as victory; the palm as a Greek pun on *phoinix* or as a sun symbol; Robinson doubted the pun).

Used for: the horse-and-palm coin at the head of Carthage's panel on the island view (the drawing is invention; the types are history), and the palm as Carthage's icon.
