# How Carthage was governed, about 410 to 380 BC (research card)

Research only. Nothing here changes game code or data. It is written for the Carthage cast (`docs/drafts/carthage-cast.md`) and for the war focus (Q3, Carthage's pendulum, in `docs/council/2026-11-27-war-and-geopolitics.md`). Every section is `kind: history` unless it says `kind: invention`.

**The short answer.** No text describes Carthage's government in our years. Diodorus tells what Carthage did from 410 to 380, and names its bodies only in passing: a senate that hears envoys, a council and then the people who hear a letter, a man who "lawfully" holds "sovereign power", later a "king" who leads the army. Every account of how those bodies fitted together is later than our years:

| Text | Written | Describes | What it gives for 410 to 380 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diodorus 13 to 15 | 60 to 30 BC, from older histories (he names Ephorus and Timaeus at 14.54.5 to 14.54.6) | our years, year by year | events only: who was chosen, who heard what, who came home how |
| Aristotle, *Politics* 2.11 | about 336 (Hoyos), so 45 to 75 years after our years | Carthage in his own day | the only description of the whole system; nothing dated |
| Justin 19 to 20 | 2nd century AD or later, a short version of Trogus (under Augustus) | Carthage from Mago (500s) to Dionysius' death (367) | the founding of the hundred judges (before our years), Himilco's homecoming (396), Suniatus and the ban on Greek (date disputed) |
| Polybius 6.51 | about 150 BC | Carthage at the start of Hannibal's war, about 218 | "kings", Elders and people; useful only as a late contrast |
| Plutarch, *Timoleon* 22 | about AD 100 | 343 or so | the first general's body punished at home, 40 years after our years |

So read every rule below as *how Greeks and Romans later said Carthage worked*, unless the line cites Diodorus 13 to 15. Where a rule is later than our years, the section says so.

**Sources.** Every quote was checked on 2026-11-27 against the saved texts in the Phoenician-Punic World repo (read-only): Diodorus 13 to 15 (Loeb, Oldfather; the Greek of 13.43.5 and 14.54.5 from the saved Greek file), Justin 17 to 20 (Watson, 1853, saved from attalus.org), Aristotle *Politics* 2.11 and 5 (Rackham), Polybius 6.51 to 6.52 (Shuckburgh), Plutarch *Timoleon* (Perrin), Livy 33.46 (Foster), Hoyos 2006, Devereaux's blog post on Carthage's generals, and six saved Wikipedia articles. No whole book was read; each was searched and the passage opened. Wikipedia is used only for what modern scholars say, and says so.

**Dates** are Diodorus' archon years, which straddle two of our years, so every "about" can be a year either way.

## 1. The top office: "king", suffete, or a general with full powers

**What the texts of our years say.**
- 410/409: Carthage chose "as general Hannibal, who at the time lawfully exercised sovereign powers" (13.43.5). The Greek says "κατὰ νόμους τότε βασιλεύοντα": being king, according to the laws, at the time. Diodorus also calls him "their foremost citizen" (13.43.5).
- 397/396: "lawfully according Himilcon sovereign power" (14.54.5). The Greek: "Ἰμίλκωνα βασιλέα κατὰ νόμον καταστήσαντες", having made Himilcon king according to the law.
- 383/382: "As general they chose their king Magon" (15.15.2). "There fell also Magon their king after a splendid combat" (15.15.3). His son replaced him "as general", not as king (15.16.2).

So for each great war of our years, Diodorus' Greek source used the word for king, twice with "according to the law". He never says how long the office lasted, how many held it, or who chose.

**What later texts say (later than our years).**
- Aristotle, about 336: "the kings and the council of Elders correspond to the kings and Elders at Sparta", and "the Carthaginian kings are not confined to the same family" (1272b). Kings and generals are elected for merit and wealth, "especially elections to the most important offices, those of the kings and of the generals" (1273a), and he complains that "the greatest offices of state, the kingship and the generalship, should be for sale" (1273a). So in his day king and general were two offices.
- Polybius, about 218: "For they had kings, and the Gerusia had the powers of an aristocracy" (6.51).
- Justin calls the Magonid Hasdrubal the holder of "eleven dictatorships" with "four triumphs" (19.1.7): Roman words for a Carthaginian office, five centuries on.

**Modern readings, side by side, not decided.**
1. **Real kings, of one family.** Beloch and Picard: "a Magonid dynasty reigning, according to him, between 550 and 370, followed by the Hannonids until 308". Wikipedia adds that "this thesis is rejected by most historians, including Maurice Sznycer" (Wikipedia, [Constitution of Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Carthage), saved copy). The saved [History of Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Carthage) article still says "The Magonid family produced several members who were elected kings between 550 BC and 370 BC".
2. **A command with full powers, for a time.** Lancel: "a provisional kingship ... a military command with full powers entrusted for a given and possibly renewable period" (Wikipedia, Constitution of Carthage).
3. **The "king" is the suffete, misnamed by Greeks.** "Erroneous references to Carthaginian "kings" with the Latin term rex betray the translations of Roman authors from Greek sources, who equated the sufet with the more monarchical basileus" (Wikipedia, [Shophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shophet), saved copy).
4. **Suffetes who sometimes also led armies.** Devereaux: "even into the 400s and early 300s, sufetes sometimes show up also acting as generals", while "By the third century the offices appear well and truly separate" ([ACOUP, Raising Carthaginian Armies, Part III](https://acoup.blog/2026/05/01/collections-raising-carthaginian-armies-part-iii-generals-warlords-and-vassals/), saved copy). He gives the Punic word for general: "the Punic term for which was rab mahanet".
5. **De facto rule by one family, inside a republic.** Hoyos: "That Carthage was a republic, at any rate from around the sixth century on, with elected leaders and an elite effectively but fluidly oligarchic", and Mago "used his prestige to establish himself and his descendants as de facto rulers of Carthage for 150 years, manipulating her republican institutions to maintain power" (Hoyos 2006, pp. 41 to 42).

**When the suffetes began.** The saved Wikipedia article contradicts itself: "Two eponymous annual suffetes existed from the 5th century BC onwards", with Amadasi Guzzo putting them "in the last third of the century", but also "The suffetate is attested at the end of the 4th century BC". [Ancient Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Carthage) has them "by at least the late fifth century BC" as "non-hereditary officials elected annually". So two yearly suffetes in 405 is possible but not shown by any text of our years. `docs/research/carthage-chronicle.md` already marks suffete-year dating as not checked.

## 2. The Elders (the senate, the council)

**Our years.**
- 410/409: Segesta's envoys "laid before the Senate the instructions the people had given them"; the Carthaginians were "in no little quandary", eager for the city but afraid of Syracuse, until Hannibal "also advised them to acquire the city" (13.43.4 to 13.43.5).
- 406/405: the Carthaginians "voted to prepare great armaments" (13.80.1), then sent "certain citizens who were held in high esteem among the Carthaginians with large sums of money, some to Iberia and others to the Baliarides Islands" to hire men (13.80.2).
- 398/397: Dionysius' herald brought a letter "to the senate". "When it had been read in the council and subsequently before the people", Carthage was distressed, "for the plague had killed great numbers of them, and they were also totally unprepared"; they "dispatched members of the senate with large sums of money to recruit mercenaries in Europe" (14.47.1 to 14.47.3).
- 383/382: after Cabala the Carthaginians asked for a truce "in order that they might discuss the question with their government" (15.16.1).

So in our years a council receives foreign letters and envoys first, the people hear them next, and senators themselves carry the silver abroad to hire armies.

**Later than our years.** Aristotle: the Elders should be "chosen from these rather than by age" (the distinguished families), "for as they are put in control of important matters, if they are men of no value they do great harm" (1272b; the saved text has a gap just before). Polybius: "the Gerusia had the powers of an aristocracy" (6.51).

**Modern readings.** How many sat, and whether there was an inner council of thirty, is answered only from much later Roman sources. The saved [Carthaginian Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthaginian_Senate) article gives a council of "30 aristocratic and noble peoples who had the ability to declare war" and a senate "created in c. 450 BC", but cites no ancient text and muddles its terms. Do not lean on it. A council of thirty appears in Roman accounts of about 200 BC; none was opened here.

## 3. The Hundred and Four, the hundred judges, and the boards of five

**Our years.** Nothing. No text of 410 to 380 names the Hundred and Four, the boards of five, or any court.

**Justin (late, and placing it before our years).** In the time of Mago's grandsons (the middle 400s): "as so numerous a family of commanders was dangerous to the liberty of the state, since they themselves managed and decided everything, a hundred judges were chosen out of the senate, ... who were to demand of the generals, when they returned from war, an account of their proceedings, in order that, under this control, they might exercise their command in war with a regard to the judicature and laws at home" (19.2.5 to 19.2.6). So in Justin the court was made to curb one family, out of the senate, to question generals when they came home. That is the closest any text comes to generals judged on return, and it is five centuries after the fact.

**Aristotle (later).** "the magistracy of the Hundred and Four corresponding to the Ephors (except one point of superiority—the Ephors are drawn from any class, but the Carthaginians elect this magistracy by merit)" (1272b). And: "The appointment by co-optation of the Boards of Five which control many important matters, and the election by these boards of the supreme magistracy of the Hundred", with long terms, "are oligarchical features"; "the members of the Boards are the judges in all lawsuits" (1273a). He does not say the Hundred judged generals.

**Is Justin's hundred Aristotle's Hundred and Four?** Most modern accounts take them as one body, but no ancient text joins them. Kept apart here.

**When was it founded? Four answers, side by side.**
1. Justin: under Mago's grandsons, so the middle 400s, before our years (19.2.5).
2. Wikipedia, Constitution of Carthage: "The Hundred and Four are said to have been set up in 396 as a tribunal for political affairs." That would be the year of Himilcon's disaster. The article gives no ancient source for 396.
3. Wikipedia, [Hundred and Four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_and_Four): "They were created early in Carthage's history".
4. Wikipedia, [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage): "From about 550 to 450 the Magonid family monopolized the top military position", and "Eventually it came to be that, after a war, the commanding general had to testify justifying his actions before a court of 104 judges."

**What the court did to generals (all later than our years).** Devereaux: generals served "until they failed badly enough to be sacked from command" (done, he says, by the "court of one-hundred and four"); "unsuccessful generals tended to be put on trial and crucified for their failure". The examples are from the 340s on: after Mago's defeat about 343, "the Carthaginians, after Mago's suicide, had impaled his dead body, in their rage at his conduct of the expedition" (Plutarch, *Timoleon* 22). Wikipedia's Constitution article gives the Elders, not the Hundred and Four, "the punishment of defeated generals". Who judged is itself disputed. By 196 the judges sat for life: Livy says the order of judges "at that time was in control in Carthage, principally because the same men were judges for life", and "The property, reputation, and life of every citizen were in their hands" (33.46.1 to 33.46.2).

## 4. The people's assembly

**Our years.** One clear case: the letter of 398/397 was read "in the council and subsequently before the people" (14.47.2). And 410/409, the envoys bring "the instructions the people had given them", but those are Segesta's people, not Carthage's (13.43.4).

**Later than our years.** Aristotle: "The reference of some matters and not of others to the popular assembly rests with the kings in consultation with the Elders in case they agree unanimously, but failing that, these matters also lie with the people"; and once there, "the people have the sovereign decision, and anybody who wishes may speak against the proposals introduced" (1273a). He also calls Carthage a state "which has a democratic government" (*Politics* 5, 1316b). Polybius, for about 218: "the multitude were supreme in such things as affected them", and "In Carthage therefore the influence of the people in the policy of the state had already risen to be supreme" (6.51). So the people's weight grows from Aristotle to Polybius, and our years come before both.

The game already uses Aristotle's rule: Carthage's goal is decided by the council when the Elders agree, otherwise by the people's assembly. That rule is about 60 years later than 396.

## 5. Generals: chosen, sent, brought home

**Chosen.** The council and people chose the general for each war (13.43.5, 13.80.1, 14.54.5, 15.15.2). In 406/405 Hannibal "begged to be excused because of his age" and they "appointed besides him another general, Himilcon, the son of Hanno and of the same family" (13.80.1). In 383/382 Magon's son, "though he was young indeed, was full of ambition", took his dead father's place (15.16.2). Aristotle, later, says the generalship was elected for wealth as well as merit and could be bought (1273a).

**Sent with whom.** Citizens served in the big wars: Hannibal "enrolled not a few from among the citizens" (13.44.6); in 383/382 "they enrolled as soldiers capable youth from their own citizens" (15.15.2). The rest were hired or levied: "five thousand Libyans and eight hundred Campanians" (13.44.1). Polybius, later: "they employ foreign mercenaries, the Romans native and citizen levies" (6.52).

**Brought home: the three homecomings of our years.**
1. **Hannibal, 409, the victor.** "when he arrived at Carthage with much booty, the whole city came out to meet him, paying him homage and honour as one who in a brief time had performed greater deeds than any general before him" (13.62.6).
2. **Himilcon, 396, the loser.** He bought his citizens' escape for "three hundred talents" and left the rest of the army (14.75.1, 14.75.4); he "slipped away in disgrace to Carthage with a few survivors" (14.76.3). Justin: "the bereaved general came out of his ship, with his belt removed, and in a mean dress like that of a slave" (19.3.1), walked through the mourning city, and "locking his door and admitting no one, not even his sons, to his presence, he put an end to his life" (19.3.11). Diodorus: "In the end he passed sentence of death upon himself and starved himself to death" (14.76.4). **No trial is mentioned** by either. He judged himself.
3. **Magon, 383/382, dead in the field.** The city "gave their king Magon a magnificent funeral" (15.16.2).

**Before our years.** Hannibal's father Gescon "had been exiled because of his father's defeat and had ended his life in Selinus" (13.43.5): the family paid for Himera (480) with exile, not a court.

So no general of 410 to 380 is recorded as tried. The sources show three ends: welcome, self-judgement, funeral. Trials and crucifixions belong to later centuries (section 3).

## 6. Money and office

Later than our years, all from Aristotle. Rulers are chosen "not only for their merit but also for their wealth" (1273a); the top offices are "for sale" (1273a); "it is probable that those who purchase office will learn by degrees to make a profit out of it" (1273b); holding "several offices" at once "is considered a distinction at Carthage" (1273b); and in Carthage, "which has a democratic government, the magistrates go in for business" (*Politics* 5, 1316b). He also names "the common mess-tables of its Comradeships" (1272b), a club life of the citizens.

In our years: silver buys armies (13.80.2, 14.47.3), Himilcon's camp held "three hundred talents" (14.75.1), and in 396 "some of them were bought off with Carthaginian money" (14.77.6, the Libyan leaders).

## 7. One family at the top

Justin's family tree, with Diodorus beside it:
- Mago (500s) had "two sons, Hasdrubal and Hamilcar" (Justin 19.1.1).
- Hamilcar, killed in Sicily (480), left "three sons, Himilco, Hanno, and Gisco"; Hasdrubal "had the same number of sons, Hannibal, Hasdrubal, and Sapho"; "By these the affairs of the Carthaginians were managed at this period" (19.2.1 to 19.2.3).
- Diodorus: Hannibal of 410 was "the grandson of Hamilcar" and "the son of Gescon" (13.43.5). Himilcon was "the son of Hanno and of the same family" (13.80.1).

Put together (my inference, not a text): Gisco's son Hannibal and Hanno's son Himilcon were cousins, grandsons of the Hamilcar who died at Himera. If so, the family that Justin's court was made to curb still led both great wars of 409 to 396. Justin himself makes Himilco a *son* of Hamilcar and the general of 396 (19.2.7), a generation off from Diodorus. Hoyos, for his part, calls the 410 war's leader "another Magonid, Hamilcar's grandson Hannibal" (p. 42) and has the Magonids rule "for 150 years". Whether Magon of 392 to 383 was of the same family, no text says.

## 8. Libya, the subjects, and the poor

**Our years.** In 405 the plague came home: "the pestilence continued to rage no less in Libya also and great numbers both of the Carthaginians themselves and of their allies were struck down" (13.114.2). In 396 the Libyan allies, "who had long hated the oppressive rule of the Carthaginians and even more at this time because of the betrayal of the soldiers at Syracuse, were inflamed against them" (14.77.1), raised "a body of two hundred thousand men" and seized Tunis (14.77.3), then broke apart: "they quarrelled among themselves over the supreme command and some of them were bought off with Carthaginian money" (14.77.6). The number is Diodorus'; read it as a very large rising.

**Just after our years, or well after.** Diodorus 15.24 (archon Nicon): a plague at Carthage "so violent" that "the Libyans, undervaluing them, seceded, and the Sardinians ... revolted"; then "turmoils and fears and panicky disturbances constantly occurred throughout the city", with men rushing out armed and fighting "one another". The saved file's heading dates this 368/367. Nicon is usually put at 379/378 (from memory; not checked in a saved list). Either way it is after 380.

**Before our years.** The Magonids had made "the Africans" "remit the tribute paid for the building of the city" (Justin 19.2.4): by the 400s Carthage no longer paid the Libyans for its ground.

**Later than our years.** Aristotle: "they constantly send out a portion of the common people toappointments in the cities" (the saved text runs two words together), which "heal the social sore", but "suppose some misfortune occurs and the multitude of the subject class revolts, there is no remedy provided by the laws" (1273b). 396 is exactly such a revolt, 60 years before he wrote.

## 9. Gods, Greeks and the Greek tongue

**Our years.** After 396 Carthage "voted by every means to propitiate the gods" (14.77.4): it brought in the cult of Demeter and Kore, "appointed their most renowned citizens to be priests of these goddesses" and "chose out the most prominent Greeks who lived among them" to serve the rites (14.77.5). In 406 Himilcon supplicated the gods at Acragas "by sacrificing a young boy to Cronus" (13.86.3). Greeks lived at Carthage and Carthaginians at Syracuse: in 398/397 "no small number of Carthaginians had their homes in Syracuse and rich possessions" (14.46.1), and the Syracusans plundered them.

**Date disputed.** Justin: the leader of a new Carthaginian expedition was "Hanno the Carthaginian, ... whose enemy Suniatus, the most powerful of the Carthaginians at that time", wrote to Dionysius "in a letter written in Greek, of the approach of the army and the inactivity of its leader", and "was found, through the letter being intercepted, guilty of treason"; then the senate decreed "that no Carthaginian should thenceforward study the Greek literature or language" (20.5.11 to 20.5.13). Justin puts this after the Gauls "who had burned Rome some months before" came to Dionysius (20.5.4), so in his order about 387 to 383. Wikipedia's Constitution article dates the ban to 368 ("prohibiting the use of Greek in 368"). And Diodorus names Magon, not Hanno, as the general of the war of 383/382 (15.15.2). Kept as an open question: the story may fall in our years or 15 years after.

## 10. Abroad

Hoyos: "In 406 BC, at the very time that she was making bitter war on the Greeks of Sicily, an inscription records a friendly alliance with Athens" (p. 41). He cites Bengtson's collection of treaties, no. 208. Not opened (see To fetch).

## Where the sources disagree or are thin

1. **King or not.** Diodorus' Greek says "king" for Hannibal (410/409), Himilcon (397/396) and Magon (383/382). Aristotle's kings are elected and separate from generals. Modern readings: real Magonid kings (Beloch, Picard); suffetes misnamed (the Shophet article); a temporary full command (Lancel); suffetes who sometimes commanded (Devereaux).
2. **Suffetes in 405.** "from the 5th century BC onwards" and "attested at the end of the 4th century BC", both in one saved article.
3. **The court.** Justin's "hundred judges" (middle 400s), Aristotle's "Hundred and Four" (in his day), "set up in 396" (Wikipedia, no source given), "created early" (Wikipedia). Whether they are one body is not shown.
4. **Who judged defeated generals.** The Hundred and Four (Devereaux, Wikipedia's Carthage article) or the Elders (Wikipedia's Constitution article). No text of our years shows anyone judging one.
5. **Himilcon's family.** Diodorus: son of Hanno, of Hannibal's family (13.80.1). Justin: son of Hamilcar, uncle to Hannibal's generation (19.2.1, 19.2.7).
6. **Himilcon's death.** Diodorus: starved himself (14.76.4). Justin: shut himself in and "put an end to his life" (19.3.11), method not given. Both agree he was not tried.
7. **Suniatus' war.** Justin: Hanno led it, about 387 to 383 by his order. Wikipedia: 368. Diodorus: Magon led the war of 383/382.
8. **Numbers.** 200,000 Libyans (14.77.3), 80,000 in 392 (14.95.1), and for 396 Ephorus' "more than three hundred thousand infantry" against Timaeus' Libyan troops that "did not exceed one hundred thousand", plus "an additional thirty thousand" from Sicily (14.54.5 to 14.54.6). Ranges, not facts.
9. **Errors in the saved Wikipedia copies.** The Constitution article puts Malchus in the "2nd century BC" and calls Bomilcar's coup of 308 "(5th century BC)". The Ancient Carthage article puts Himilco's siege of Syracuse in "387 BC" (Diodorus: 396). Use those articles for modern views only.

## Considered and left out

- **Malchus** (Justin 18.7): exiled with his army, his son crucified, ten senators killed, then put to death for "aspiring to be king". About 550, legend, and long before our years.
- **Bomilcar's coup of 308** and **Hanno the Great** (in power from the 360s; Aristotle's "Hanno at Carthage", *Politics* 5, 1307a, is undated): later than our years. The game's invented Bomilcar is not the man of 308.
- **Hannibal's reform of the judges** (Livy 33.46, 196 BC) and **the council of thirty** (Roman accounts of about 200, not opened): far later.
- **Diodorus 20.43 to 20.44** (308, saved in Greek): later.
