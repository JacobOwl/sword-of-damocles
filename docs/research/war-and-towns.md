# War and the towns: the card for the war and geopolitics focus

**Dates.** Diodorus counts by archon years, so give ranges. The war of the 380s (Cabala, Cronium) is about 383 to 375 and outside the 405 to 395 game: it is used as a **pattern** for peace terms and battle, never as an event in the decade.

**What is invention.** Every number, every track, and how each rule works.

## The rhythm of the wars

- "They're then back at it from 409 to 405, then again from 398 to 396, then again from 383 to 381 (?), then again from 368 to 367 ... at any given time in these wars, territorial control often swings wildly between Carthage and Syracuse, but on the other hand zooming out, over the long-term relatively little changes ... the settlements in the middle either manage in the fracture-zone between the powers or submit to one or the other" (Devereaux, ACOUP, "Raising Carthaginian Armies" part 1, 2026; a modern view).
- Wars begin when the other side is weak. Dionysius went to war when "many Carthaginians in Libya had fallen victims to a plague" (14.41.1). Carthage, "totally unprepared" (14.47.2), recruited first.
- The declaration had a demand: "the Syracusans had resolved to make war upon the Carthaginians unless they withdrew from the Greek cities" (14.47.2).
- A pretext from defecting towns (380s pattern): Dionysius, "seeing ... that the cities subject to the Carthaginians were favourable to a revolt, he received such as wished to do so"; "The Carthaginians at first dispatched ambassadors to the ruler and asked for the return of their cities, and when he paid no attention to them, this came to be the beginning of the war" (15.15.1-2).
- Recovery is slow: "The Carthaginians, after a slow recovery from the disaster they had suffered at Syracuse, resolved to keep their hand in Sicilian affairs" (14.95.1). In between, Magon "showed kindness to the subject cities and received the victims of Dionysius' wars" (14.90.3).

## Peace terms

- 392 (just after the decade): "The conditions were like the former except that the Siceli were to be subject to Dionysius and that he was to receive Tauromenium" (14.96.4).
- The victor's demand after Cabala (380s pattern): "his only terms were conditional upon their retiring from the cities of Sicily and paying the cost of the war" (15.15.4).
- The truce as a trap: they "asked Dionysius to agree to a truce of a few days", and in it the new general "spent the entire period of the truce drilling and exercising his troops" (15.16.1-2).
- The reversal: "the Carthaginians received both the city of the Selinuntians and its territory and that of Acragas as far as the river called Halycus. And Dionysius paid the Carthaginians one thousand talents" (15.17.5).
- The secret bargain (already a game event): Dionysius let Carthage's citizens escape "in order that the Syracusans, by reason of their fear of the Carthaginians, should never find a time of ease to assert their freedom" (14.75.3).

## Towns' own politics

- Generals who sell the town: Arcesilaus of Catana "had offered to betray the city to him"; Procles of Naxos was "won over by great promises" (14.15.1-2).
- A general who would not: Phyton of Rhegium "cried out that he was punished because he would not betray the city to Dionysius" (14.112.3); Dionysius had him flogged through the city "because he had persuaded the city to undertake the war" (14.112.2).
- News of a defeat turns towns: after Dionysius' failed attack on Tauromenium, "the Acragantini and Messenians banished the partisans of Dionysius, asserted their freedom, and renounced their alliance with the tyrant" (14.88.5).
- A divided town: at Messana "they could not agree among themselves about the war" (14.56.3); one party "removed from the city their children and wives and most valuable possessions to neighbouring cities" (14.56.4); the city fell while its men were out guarding the landing (14.57.1-3).
- A town's assembly punishes its generals (406, just before the decade): at Acragas the crowd, "vexed that the opportunity had been let slip", heard an accusation and "began to throw stones and killed four of them" (13.87.4-5).
- Hostages hold an unwilling ally: "since the Campanians had given hostages to Dionysius ... they were compelled to maintain the alliance with Dionysius, although they would gladly have joined the Carthaginians" (14.61.6).
- A local lord who feeds one side and starves the other: Agyris "readily provided the entire army of Dionysius with food" (14.95.7); Magon, "encamped in hostile territory and ... ever more and more in want of supplies", while Agyris' men "were continually cutting off the enemy's supplies" (14.96.1).
- Towns going over and away: the Halicyaeans "deserted him and sent ambassadors to the Carthaginian camp" once he left (14.55.7).
- Polyaenus 5.2.18 (2nd century AD, a late and doubtful anecdote): Dionysius spared certain men's estates while ravaging so the Messenians would suspect them. Use only labelled "doubtful".

## Campaigns, supply, ravaging

- Supply decides battle: "Although the Sicilian Greeks were eager for a battle, Dionysius conceived it to be better, both because he was widely separated from his allied cities and because the transport of his food supplies was reduced, to renew the war in other areas" (14.55.5); he went home "laying waste the territory though which he led his army" (14.55.7).
- Ravaging: Dionysius "plundered the territory of Solûs and Panormus, and that also of Halicyae, and cut down the trees on it" (14.48.5). Himilcon "for thirty days overran the countryside, cutting down the trees and laying it all waste ... to satisfy the soldiers with every kind of plunder, but also to reduce the besieged to despair" (14.62.5).
- Refusing battle costs allies: the Sicilian Greeks, "angered at his unwillingness to encounter the enemy, deserted Dionysius" (14.61.3); in 392 Dionysius said "time and want would ruin the barbarians without fighting. Provoked to anger at this the Syracusans deserted him" (14.96.2).
- A victory without the tyrant is dangerous to him: the Syracusans, "without his presence, had won a victory over the Carthaginians" and "were now puffed up with pride" (14.64.3); Theodorus: "put an end not only to the Phoenician war but to the tyrant within our walls" (14.65.3).
- Unpaid mercenaries (406): they "threatened to go over to the enemy" (13.88.2-3, `book13.loeb.txt`).
- Leading in person: at Tauromenium Dionysius "barely escaped being taken alive" (14.88.3).

## Battle

- A three-part plan that failed (Gela, 405): Dionysius "divided his infantry into three groups" (13.109.4); parts "came too late" (13.110.4).
- A death on a wing: Leptines "ended his life in a blaze of glory" on one wing; "when the death of Leptines became known and the other wing was crushed, his men were dismayed and took to flight" (15.17.1-2, 380s pattern). At Cabala, after Magon fell, Carthage "at once sent an embassy to discuss terms of peace" (15.15.3-4).
- The camp and night: "The survivors, who found safety in the camp, were preserved by the coming of night" (15.17.4).
- Spending men you distrust: Dionysius sent ahead the mercenaries who had "engaged time and again in factional quarrels" and had the cavalry "flee and leave the mercenaries in the lurch" (14.72.2-3).
- Rashness: Leptines at sea, "in no cowardly fashion, but without prudence" (14.60.2).

## Silent or weak

- No grain amounts, pay rates or march speeds for the decade; army numbers are inflated and disputed (14.54.5-6).
- The Libyan revolt (14.77.1-3) is saved in Greek only; not quoted.
- Nothing on how a town held by Carthage governed itself.
- Battle tactics beyond wings, rout and camp are not in Diodorus for this period; don't import Hannibal-era tactics.
