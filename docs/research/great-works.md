# Great works: the Epipolae wall, the arms workshops and the great fleet

kind: history (the works) and invention (every rule and number). For the switch "greatWorks" (bet B4 of `docs/council/2026-11-23-two-day-plan.md`), built 2026-11-23 by thread 23. Data in `data/works.json`, rules in `src/core/works.ts`.

Every quote below was opened and copied from the saved Loeb text (C. H. Oldfather, 1954, via LacusCurtius). The sections are copied whole into `sources/diodorus/book14-works.loeb.excerpts.txt` in this repo, from the phoenician-punic-world project's `sources/diodorus/english/` files.

## The idea

The council's Historian seat: much of Dionysius' power in about 402 to 397 came from building. Diodorus gives three great works in a row: the Epipolae wall (14.18), the arms workshops (14.41 to 14.43) and the fleet with its ship-sheds (14.42). They took labour, silver, timber and craftsmen from other places, and each one turned into power he could use: a city that could not be cut off, engines for sieges, armour for the guard, a fleet "double his in number". In the game they are objects built over seasons, not one-off choices.

## Dates (ranges, not one year)

- **The Epipolae wall: about 402 to 401.** Diodorus puts it under the year of 14.17 to 14.18. `docs/research/events-405-395.md` already dates it "about 402/401". The archon years start about midsummer and Diodorus sometimes misplaces Sicilian events (`docs/research/calendar.md`), so treat this as a range.
- **The arms and the fleet: about 399 to 398.** Under the year of 14.38 (Aristocrates archon) and finished "as soon as the major part of the task of making arms and building a fleet was completed" (14.44.1), at the start of the next year. The war follows in 397.

The game does not fix these dates: a work can be begun in any season once the switch is on. That is deliberate (history as conditions, not a script).

## The Epipolae wall (Diodorus 14.18)

| What | Section | Quote |
|---|---|---|
| Why: never to be cut off from the land again | 14.18.2 | "he took care that he should never, when caught at a similar disadvantage, be cut off from contact with the countryside; for he saw that the site of Epipolae, as it is called, naturally commanded the city of the Syracusans" |
| The labour: peasants from the countryside | 14.18.4 | "he gathered the peasants from the countryside, from whom he selected some sixty thousand capable men and parcelled out to them the space to be walled" |
| The organisation | 14.18.5 | "For each stade he appointed a master-builder and for each plethron a mason, and the labourers from the common people assigned to the task numbered two hundred for each plethron" |
| Oxen and stone | 14.18.5 | "six thousand yoke of oxen brought it to the appointed place" |
| Prizes, and the ruler on site | 14.18.6 | "he in person, together with his friends, oversaw the work through all the days required, visiting every section and ever lending a hand to the toilers" |
| He worked as one of them | 14.18.7 | "he laid aside the dignity of his office and reduced himself to the ranks" |
| Twenty days | 14.18.8 | "contrary to expectation, the wall was brought to completion in twenty days" |
| Its size and strength | 14.18.8 | "It was thirty stades in length and of corresponding height, and the added strength of the wall made it impregnable to assault" |

**Numbers to be careful with.** "Sixty thousand" men, "six thousand yoke of oxen", "twenty days" and "thirty stades" are Diodorus' numbers; whether they are right is a question for the historians (a stade is about 180 m, so 30 stades is roughly 5 to 6 km; the surviving Epipolae circuit is often described as longer, which suggests the twenty-day wall was one stretch, not the whole circuit; **unverified here, not opened**). The game uses none of them as rules: the wall takes one season of work.

## The arms workshops (Diodorus 14.41 to 14.43)

| What | Section | Quote |
|---|---|---|
| The craftsmen, from three places | 14.41.3 | "he gathered skilled workmen, commandeering them from the cities under his control and attracting them by high wages from Italy and Greece as well as Carthaginian territory" |
| The aim | 14.41.3 | "to make weapons in great numbers and every kind of missile, and also quadriremes and quinqueremes" |
| Overseers from the leading citizens | 14.41.4 | "appointed over them the most conspicuous citizens, offering great bounties to any who created a supply of arms" |
| The whole city a workshop | 14.41.6 | "every space, such as the porticoes and back rooms of the temples as well as the gymnasia and colonnades of the market place, crowded with workers" |
| The catapult | 14.42.1 | "the catapult was invented at this time in Syracuse, since the ablest skilled workmen had been gathered from everywhere into one place" |
| He went among the workmen | 14.42.1 | "Dionysius circulated daily among the workers, conversed with them in kindly fashion, and rewarded the most zealous with gifts and invited them to his table" |
| What was made | 14.43.2 | "one hundred and forty thousand shields and a like number of daggers and helmets" ... "corselets were made ready ... more than fourteen thousand in number" |
| For whom | 14.43.3 | "to distribute to his cavalry and the commanders of the infantry, as well as to the mercenaries who were to form his bodyguard" |
| The catapults in use, at Motya | 14.50.4 | "the Syracusans slew many of the enemy by using from the land the catapults which shot sharp-pointed missiles. Indeed this weapon created great dismay, because it was a new invention at this time" |

**Disagreement to show, not settle.** Whether the catapult was really invented at Syracuse in 399 rests mostly on this passage; modern accounts of early artillery discuss it (**not opened here**). The game says only what Diodorus says, and the event "The arms workshops" already says the same.

| Ships and ship-sheds | 14.42.5 | "he began at one and the same time to build more than two hundred ships and to refit the one hundred and ten he already had; and he also constructed about the Great Harbour, as it is now called, one hundred and sixty costly ship-sheds, and repaired the one hundred and fifty which were already there" |

Earlier, the citadel on the Island already held dockyards (14.7.3: "within its wall he enclosed the dockyards which are connected with the small harbour that is known as Laccium. The dockyards could accommodate sixty triremes"). The citadel is the old "Build" action and is not one of these works.

## What is invention

All of it except the works themselves and the facts quoted above:
- The season counts (wall 1, arms 3, fleet 3), all silver and grain costs, and "two works at once".
- The five kinds of labour and their prices. The three sources of craftsmen are from 14.41.3; the choice between peasants and hired labour for the wall is invented (Diodorus has only the peasants).
- The peasants missing the summer harvest, and the citizens' resentment. Diodorus says the opposite of resentment for the wall ("all were zealous", 14.18.6), but that was with the ruler on site and prizes offered; the game makes your presence the thing that turns the work into goodwill.
- Allies resenting commandeered craftsmen, and Carthage's anger at the craftsmen lured from its lands and at the finished arms and fleet. Diodorus does not say how Carthage heard of the works.
- An overseer who is no friend of yours skimming the silver; an overseer's faction pleased; the overseer gaining standing when the work is done (14.41.4 says only that the leading citizens were set over the workmen).
- What each work gives: walls +1; engines +1 to storm walls (the existing "armed" flag); guard strength +1; Syracuse's own defenders +1; the fleet's 2 months of grain each autumn.
- The fleet's effect on Carthage's crossing (the interception roll) is not built yet: the roll is in `src/core/carthage.ts`, which a test-build stream owns until it merges. See `docs/handoff.md`.
