## Dates

- Death of Agathocles: 289 BC, from the modern Wikipedia extract only, which says he was "a tyrant of Syracuse from 317 BC and king of much of Sicily from 304 BC until his death" with life dates given there as 361 to 289 BC. A BC year for his death is not in the saved ancient texts.
- The saved Diodorus gives a reign length instead: "He lived for seventy-two years and ruled for twenty-eight" (Diodorus 21.16.5), on the authority of Timaeus, Callias and Antander.
- Range for the start: 289 to 288 BC, the months and first two years after his death. The whole chain in this dossier runs further: Hicetas "had ruled Syracuse for nine years" (Diodorus 22.7.2), so the Hicetas period is about 289 to 280 and the later events (Pyrrhus, Phintias' fall, Hiero) are outside the start window. Items from after about 285 are marked **later** below.
- The seizure of Messana by the mercenaries follows the settlement after the Menon war (Diodorus 21.18.1). The saved texts give no year for it. A range of about 288 to 283 is my reading of the order of events, not a stated date.

Game clock proposal (invention): begin on the day the king's body is burned, with the Assembly about to vote on the confiscation of his property.

## The six power objects

**Citadel.** Not named for 289 in the saved texts. The nearest evidence is **later**, during the war of the two leaders: "Thoenon controlled the Island, while Sostratus ruled Syracuse" (Diodorus 22.7.6), and Pyrrhus "accepted delivery of the Island from Thoenon, and of the rest of the city from the citizens and Sosistratus" (Diodorus 22.8.4). Proposal (invention): the Island is a separate object from the city, holdable by a different person. The saved texts show that split happening within this period, so the game should allow it from the start.

**Treasury.** Holder at the start: the restored people, by confiscation. "The Syracusans, upon the recovery of their popular government, confiscated the property of Agathocles and pulled down the statues that he had set up" (Diodorus 21.16.6). Justin has the king trying to get the movable wealth out first: he resolved on sending his wife and children to Egypt "with all his treasure, and servants, and regal furniture" (Justin 23.2.6). The amount is not in the saved texts.

**Mint.** Not in the saved texts. Proposal (invention): the mint sits with whoever holds the city, and the first crisis is paying the dead king's soldiers out of his confiscated estate.

**Fleet.** Holder at the start: unclear, and this is the loudest gap. Just before his death the king "had, fully equipped, two hundred ships, quadriremes and sexremes" (Diodorus 21.16.1), built to blockade Carthaginian grain from Sardinia and Sicily. The saved texts do not say who held those ships after he died. **Later**, Pyrrhus took over at Syracuse "one hundred and twenty decked vessels, twenty without decks, and the royal "niner"" (Diodorus 22.8.5). Proposal (invention): a standing fleet object that decays each year it is unpaid, starting large.

**Generalship.** Holder: contested from the first season. Archagathus had it by the king's own appointment: the king "had entrusted the command of his forces in the field to Archagathus" (Diodorus 21.16.2). Then Menon killed him: "he assassinated Archagathus, gained control of the camp" (Diodorus 21.16.6). Then the city appointed its own man: "The people of Syracuse dispatched Hicetas as general with an army to conduct the war against Menon" (Diodorus 21.18.1).

**Assembly.** Holder: the restored people, with the mercenaries shut out. The king on his deathbed "summoned the populace, denounced Archagathus for his impiety, aroused the masses to avenge him, and declared that he restored to the people their self-government" (Diodorus 21.16.4). The fight that follows is exactly a franchise fight: "because the mercenaries were not allowed to vote in the elections, the city was filled with civil strife" (Diodorus 21.18.1).

Seventh object worth modelling (invention, but attested here): **the Elders.** "it was only with difficulty that the Elders, after long negotiations and many appeals to both factions, ended the disturbance" (Diodorus 21.18.1). A council that can broker, but not command.

## The factions

1. **The restored citizen body.** They confiscate and smash the statues (Diodorus 21.16.6). Goal: keep the vote to themselves. They are also the party that has to give hostages: "the Syracusans were compelled to give four hundred hostages to the Phoenicians" (Diodorus 21.18.1).
2. **The mercenaries in the city.** Shut out of the vote, armed, and property-owning: the settlement required that "the mercenaries should sell their possessions and leave Sicily" (Diodorus 21.18.1). Goal: the vote, or land somewhere else.
3. **The field army at the camp.** It is a political body in its own right. Menon "gained control of the camp, and, having won over the masses by expressions of goodwill, determined to wage war on Syracuse" (Diodorus 21.16.6). Goal: pay, and a patron.
4. **The dynasty's remnant.** Archagathus the grandson, the murdered younger Agathocles, and the widow and infants sent away (Justin 23.2.6). Goal: the inheritance. Diodorus says Archagathus "resolved to lay a plot for both men" (Diodorus 21.16.3).
5. **The returning exiles.** Part of the peace terms: the Syracusans were compelled "to restore the exiles" (Diodorus 21.18.1). Goal: their confiscated property back. This collides with faction 1, which has just confiscated the king's.
6. **The Elders.** Brokers, not rulers (Diodorus 21.18.1).
7. **The city tyrants elsewhere (later).** "Throughout Sicily there were tyrants, Hicetas in Syracuse, Phintias in Acragas, Tyndarion in Tauromenium, and others in the lesser cities" (Diodorus 22.2.1). Goal: eat each other. "A war arose between Phintias and Hicetas" (Diodorus 22.2.1).
8. **The Mamertines (from the seizure onward).** A soldier-state. "the Mamertines (for this was the name which the Campanians gave themselves after they became masters of Messene)" (Polybius 1.8). Goal: tribute and land. **Later**, they "levied tribute from many parts of Sicily" (Polybius 1.8).

## Carthage's posture

Carthage is the kingmaker in the first war, then the invader.

- Backing Menon against the city: "when the Carthaginians, with their vastly superior forces, began to aid Menon, the Syracusans were compelled to give four hundred hostages to the Phoenicians" (Diodorus 21.18.1).
- Justin: "the Carthaginians, learning the state of affairs in Sicily" ... "crossed over to it with a great force, and reduced several cities" (Justin 23.2.13).
- Carthage's western zone was restored by the earlier treaty, under which "the Phoenicians should regain all the cities which had formerly been subject to them" (Diodorus 20.79.5), in return for payment to Agathocles of "gold to the value of three hundred talents of silver (or, as Timaeus says, one hundred and fifty), and two hundred thousand measures of grain" (Diodorus 20.79.5). This is about 306, so earlier than this start, but it is what defines the map.
- Grain and sea power. Agathocles had planned "to prevent the Phoenicians from importing grain from Sardinia and Sicily" (Diodorus 21.16.1), and Diodorus notes "it was by control of the sea that the Carthaginians had brought their country safely out of danger" (Diodorus 21.16.1).
- **Later**: Carthage allies with the Mamertines against Pyrrhus (Diodorus 22.7.4), then besieges Syracuse, blockading "the Great Harbour with a hundred ships" with "fifty thousand men" on land (Diodorus 22.8.1), then allies with Rome (Diodorus 22.7.5).

Game use (invention): Carthage's lever at this start is not conquest but hostages and subsidy. It can pick a side in the Syracusan quarrel at a price.

## The island

- **Syracuse**: the restored popular government, with the mercenaries armed inside it (Diodorus 21.16.6, 21.18.1).
- **The camp (near Etna at the king's death)**: "While he was encamped near Etna" (Diodorus 21.16.3), then held by Menon (Diodorus 21.16.6).
- **Messana**: Greek and friendly at the start; it welcomes the departing mercenaries "as friends and allies" and then loses the city to them (Diodorus 21.18.1). After that it is the Mamertines': "took possession of the city. They named this city Mamertina after Ares" (Diodorus 21.18.1).
- **Segesta**: destroyed by Agathocles and resettled with his own soldiers. He "changed the name to Dicaeopolis and gave it as dwelling to the deserters" (Diodorus 20.71.5). Menon the poisoner came from there (Diodorus 21.16.2).
- **The Carthaginian west**: Heraclea, Selinus, Himera, Eryx, Panormus, Lilybaeum are all shown with Carthaginian garrisons or Carthaginian control in the **later** Pyrrhus narrative (Diodorus 22.10.2, 22.10.3, 22.10.4, 22.10.5), and the treaty of about 306 had restored "all the cities which had formerly been subject to them" (Diodorus 20.79.5).
- **Acragas (later)**: Phintias, then Sosistratus. "Phintias in Acragas" (Diodorus 22.2.1); then Pyrrhus "arrived at Acragas and took over from Sosistratus the city and the soldiers" (Diodorus 22.10.1).
- **Gela (later)**: destroyed by Phintias. "He tore down the walls and houses of Gela, and transferred its people to Phintias" (Diodorus 22.2.2).
- **Tauromenium (later)**: "Tyndarion in Tauromenium" (Diodorus 22.2.1).
- **Leontini (later)**: "envoys arrived from Leontini, sent by Heracleides the ruler" (Diodorus 22.8.5).
- **Enna (later)**: it had a Carthaginian garrison kept "to prevent Phintias from becoming their ruler" (Diodorus 22.10.1).
- **Agyrium (later)**: first to revolt from Phintias: "the first to revolt being the people of Agyrium" (Diodorus 22.2.3).
- **In Italy**: Croton was taken and garrisoned by Agathocles (Diodorus 21.4.1); the Bruttians broke free, "recovered the hostages, and so freed themselves from the domination of Agathocles" (Diodorus 21.8.1). Rhegium falls to its own Campanian garrison **later** (Diodorus 22.1.3; Polybius 1.7).

## People (17)

History first, each with at least one exact quote. Role lines are my summary.

1. **Agathocles** (history). The dying king. "declared that he restored to the people their self-government" (Diodorus 21.16.4). Reign: "He lived for seventy-two years and ruled for twenty-eight" (Diodorus 21.16.5).
2. **Archagathus** (history). Grandson, commander of the field army, assassin of the heir, then victim. "He was the son of the Archagathus who was killed in Libya, and thus the grandson of King Agathocles" (Diodorus 21.16.3). "In manliness and fortitude Archagathus was much in advance of his years, for he was extremely young" (Diodorus 21.16.7).
3. **Agathocles the younger** (history). The named heir, murdered at a feast. The king wished "to promote his son Agathocles as successor to the throne" (Diodorus 21.16.3); Archagathus "plied him with drink, and murdered him during the night" (Diodorus 21.16.3).
4. **Menon of Segesta** (history). The king's slave and favourite, his poisoner, then warlord of the camp. "There was a certain Menon, a Segestan by birth, who was taken captive on the seizure of his native city" (Diodorus 21.16.2). "Menon gave him one that he had smeared with a putrefactive drug" (Diodorus 21.16.4).
5. **Hicetas** (history). The city's general against Menon, later its ruler. "The people of Syracuse dispatched Hicetas as general with an army to conduct the war against Menon" (Diodorus 21.18.1). "Hicetas had ruled Syracuse for nine years when he was thrust from power by Thoenon" (Diodorus 22.7.2).
6. **Oxythemis** (history). Envoy of King Demetrius, present at the death. "Oxythemis, the envoy of King Demetrius, placed him on the pyre and burned him, still alive" (Diodorus 21.16.5).
7. **Texena** (history; Justin's spelling). The king's wife, sent to Egypt with the children and the treasure. He resolved "on embarking his wife Texena, and two infant sons that he had by her, with all his treasure" (Justin 23.2.6). She argued against going: "by marrying him, she not only engaged to share his good fortune, but all his fortune" (Justin 23.2.8).
8. **Antander** (history). The king's brother, and a historian of him. Diodorus names "Antander, the brother of Agathocles, who was himself a historian" (Diodorus 21.16.5).
9. **Timaeus of Syracuse** (history). Exiled historian, the hostile source. "he was banished from Sicily by Agathocles and could not strike back while the monarch lived" (Diodorus 21.17.1).
10. **Callias** (history). The paid historian. He "has never ceased singing dishonest praises of his paymaster" (Diodorus 21.17.4).
11. **Lanassa** (history). The king's daughter, married to Pyrrhus, and the hook for the later intervention. Agathocles said "that he was escorting his daughter Lanassa with royal honours to Epirus for her marriage" (Diodorus 21.4.1). Later the Syracusans "pinned their hopes on Pyrrhus because of his wife Lanassa" (Diodorus 22.8.2).
12. **Phintias of Acragas** (history, later). Tyrant, city-founder, city-destroyer. "Phintias founded a city, which he named Phintias, settling in it the inhabitants of Gela, who were driven from their homes" (Diodorus 22.2.2). "since he had shown himself a bloodthirsty murderer, all the cities subject to him came to loathe him" (Diodorus 22.2.3).
13. **Thoenon, son of Mameus** (history, later). Took the Island. "Thoenon controlled the Island, while Sostratus ruled Syracuse" (Diodorus 22.7.6).
14. **Sosistratus** (history, later). Master of Acragas and of an army. "This Sosistratus had made himself master of Acragas and of many other cities, and had an army of more than ten thousand men" (Diodorus 22.8.4).
15. **Tyndarion of Tauromenium** (history, later). The tyrant who let Pyrrhus in. "Tyndarion, the tyrant of Tauromenia, inclined in favour of Pyrrhus" (Diodorus 22.7.4).
16. **Hiero** (history, later; outside the start window). Elected by soldiers, then king. Polybius: the soldiers "elected commanders from their own body. These were Artemidorus and Hiero" (Polybius 1.8). Justin: "Hieron was made governor of it" after Pyrrhus left (Justin 23.4.1).
17. **Decius Jubellius** (history, later). The Campanian commander who copied the Mamertines at Rhegium. "The garrison, four thousand in number, under the command of a Campanian named Decius Jubellius" (Polybius 1.7). Diodorus: "Decius, the Roman tribune, appointed to guard Rhegium because of King Pyrrhus, slaughtered the men of the city" (Diodorus 22.1.2).

Invention people, with reasons. Each Greek name below is borrowed from a real but different man in the saved texts, so the names sound right for Syracuse.

18. **A Campanian captain, Meniscus** (invention). Spokesman of the mercenaries in the franchise fight. Reason: Diodorus says the mercenaries acted as a body but names none of them at Syracuse. The name is from "Meniscus, the leader of the mercenaries" (Diodorus 20.23.6), a different man in a different war.
19. **An Elder, Demophilus** (invention). The broker who got the settlement through. Reason: the Elders are named as a body only (Diodorus 21.18.1). Name from Diodorus 19.104.2.
20. **A returning exile, Nymphodorus** (invention). Wants his family's confiscated farm back. Reason: the peace restored the exiles but names none. Name from Diodorus 19.103.2.
21. **A Carthaginian paymaster, Hanno** (invention, common Carthaginian name in the texts, e.g. "Hanno had command of the right wing", Diodorus 20.10.6). He carries the subsidy that keeps Menon's camp fed.
22. **A ship captain, Stilpo** (invention). Holds part of the two hundred ships and sells his loyalty. Reason: the fleet's fate is the biggest gap. Name from "The navy he entrusted to Stilpo" (Diodorus 21.8.1).

## What the player starts as

**Main start (invention): an officer in the camp near Etna on the night the king dies.** You serve Archagathus, who has just murdered the heir. Your choices are to back the grandson, back the poisoner, or sell the camp to the city. The sword check has plenty of warning material: a grandson who kills an heir (Diodorus 21.16.3) and a camp that kills its commander (Diodorus 21.16.6).

**Alternate start A (invention): a Campanian captain of the hired companies in Syracuse.** You have a house, a family, money in Syracusan land, and no vote: "the mercenaries were not allowed to vote in the elections" (Diodorus 21.18.1). Play the franchise fight. If you lose, you must sell up and sail, and then the road leads past Messana (Diodorus 21.18.1).

**Alternate start B (invention): a returned exile with a claim.** You come home under the peace term "to restore the exiles" (Diodorus 21.18.1), into a city that has just confiscated the king's property (Diodorus 21.16.6) and has four hundred of its children in Carthaginian hands (Diodorus 21.18.1). Play for restitution against the men who hold your land.

## The mercenary market at this date

This is the richest start for the mercenary market. Everything here has a quote.

- **No vote, so no loyalty.** "because the mercenaries were not allowed to vote in the elections, the city was filled with civil strife" ... "Both the Syracusans and the mercenaries had recourse to arms" (Diodorus 21.18.1).
- **Soldiers hold property in the city.** The settlement made them "sell their possessions and leave Sicily" within a set time (Diodorus 21.18.1). So hired men had become landlords and householders. For the game: hired companies should accumulate assets in the city they garrison, and those assets should be what makes expelling them explosive.
- **Land and houses as payment.** Agathocles had resettled a destroyed city with his own men: Segesta renamed, and given "as dwelling to the deserters" (Diodorus 20.71.5).
- **Freedom as payment.** "he freed those of their slaves who were fit for military service" (Diodorus 20.4.8). Justin: he "obliged all the slaves that were of age for war, after receiving their freedom, to take the military oath" (Justin 22.4.5).
- **Seizure of a host city.** Diodorus: "they slew their hosts in the night, married their wives, and took possession of the city" (Diodorus 21.18.1). Polybius: "they seized promiscuously the wives and children of the dispossessed citizens" and "All other property and the land they took possession of by a subsequent division and retained" (Polybius 1.7).
- **Seizure is contagious.** Polybius: "The speed with which they became masters of a fair territory and city found ready imitators of their conduct" (Polybius 1.7). Diodorus on Rhegium: "These soldiers were Campanians, and acted just as the Mamertines did, after they slaughtered the men of Messana" (Diodorus 22.1.2).
- **Unfair shares break a captain.** "his distribution of the property of the victims was unjust" and so "Decius was driven out of Rhegium and was sent into exile by his own Campanians" (Diodorus 22.1.2).
- **A soldier-state lives on tribute.** **Later**, the Mamertines "levied tribute from many parts of Sicily" (Polybius 1.8).
- **Arrears cause mutiny, and mutiny causes massacre.** Justin: "the discharge of their arrears of pay had been deferred" (Justin 22.8.4). Diodorus: soldiers "kept demanding the pay that was due them, and they elected generals to lead the army" (Diodorus 20.33.8). And when Ligurians and Etruscans "mutinously demanded their pay" from the king's son, Agathocles "put them all to death, to the number of at least two thousand" (Diodorus 21.3.1).
- **The enemy bids for your soldiers.** Carthage "promised to give them greater pay and noteworthy bonuses" (Diodorus 20.34.1).
- **Raising pay to buy a guard (not Sicily, but the same market).** At Cassandreia, Apollodorus recruited Gauls, and "by an increase in the pay of his soldiers, and by sharing his riches with the poor, he made himself master of a formidable force" (Diodorus 22.5.2). Label: Macedonia, not Sicily.
- **Soldiers elect their own commanders (later).** Polybius: the Syracusan forces "quarrelled with the citizens, and while stationed near Merganè elected commanders from their own body" (Polybius 1.8).
- **A ruler can spend soldiers deliberately (later).** Hiero "thrust to the front and allowed them to be completely cut to pieces by the foreigners" to be rid of "the mutinous and seditious element in the army" (Polybius 1.9).
- **Soldiers as a purchasable stock.** Hiero "took Mylae by storm and acquired fifteen hundred soldiers" (Diodorus 22.13.1), and Pyrrhus took from Sosistratus "eight thousand infantry and eight h" is cut off in the saved file, so the cavalry figure is not readable (Diodorus 22.10.1).

Design note (invention): model a company as a contract with four numbers (pay owed, origin, assets held in the host city, and a grudge) and three exits (paid off, settled on land, or seizes the city). The texts above give one example of each exit.

## Stories this start can make

1. **A restored republic that cannot pay its army.** The people confiscate the king's property (Diodorus 21.16.6) but the field army is loose under its own commander (Diodorus 21.16.6) and the city has to hire a general to fight it (Diodorus 21.18.1). The sim should be able to generate a city that wins the vote and loses the soldiers.
2. **The franchise fight.** "because the mercenaries were not allowed to vote in the elections, the city was filled with civil strife" (Diodorus 21.18.1). Two armed blocs, a brokered deal, and an expulsion with a deadline. The game should let the deal hold, fail, or be betrayed.
3. **The host city swallowed by its guests.** "they were welcomed by the people of Messana as friends and allies" (Diodorus 21.18.1), and then the night killing. Polybius adds the motive: "owing to its beauty and wealth" (Polybius 1.7). The sim should produce such a seizure out of a billeting decision, not an event card.
4. **The succession that eats itself.** Diodorus: the grandson plotted "for both men" (Diodorus 21.16.3), the king denounced him while dying (Diodorus 21.16.4), the poisoner then killed the grandson (Diodorus 21.16.6). Three deaths in one year, each with a visible reason.
5. **Hostages as a standing debt.** "the Syracusans were compelled to give four hundred hostages to the Phoenicians" (Diodorus 21.18.1). A hostage pool held abroad should constrain every later decision, and should be lootable, redeemable or forfeit.
6. **The invited king.** **Later**, the exhausted rivals both "sent ambassadors to Pyrrhus" (Diodorus 22.7.6), and Plutarch has Sicilians who "begged him to help them to drive out the Carthaginians and rid the island of its tyrants" (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 22). A faction should be able to import a foreign ruler, and regret it.

## Disagreements (shown, not chosen)

**Did the king die of poison or of disease?**
- Diodorus: Archagathus "persuaded him to poison the king" (Diodorus 21.16.3), and the quill was "smeared with a putrefactive drug" (Diodorus 21.16.4).
- Justin: no poison at all, only illness. "the violence of a disease which he contracted obliged him a few days after to return to Sicily" (Justin 23.2.3), and "a pestilential humour spreading through all his nerves and joints" (Justin 23.2.4).
- Wikipedia (MODERN, second-hand) notes both and says "Justinius and the majority of modern historians assert he died a natural death". Second-hand; not an ancient source.

**Who killed whom in the succession?**
- Diodorus names the grandson Archagathus as the one who murdered the young Agathocles and was then killed by Menon (Diodorus 21.16.3, 21.16.6).
- Justin gives only the bare outcome: "the grandson, after killing the son, got possession of the supreme dignity" (Justin 23.2.5). Justin does not report Menon.

**Did he restore the republic, or did it just collapse?**
- Diodorus: a deliberate deathbed act, "he restored to the people their self-government" (Diodorus 21.16.4), followed by confiscation (Diodorus 21.16.6).
- Justin: the dying king's concern is his family's escape, not the constitution (Justin 23.2.6). Then "the Carthaginians, learning the state of affairs in Sicily" invaded (Justin 23.2.13).

**What kind of man is being remembered?**
- Diodorus condemns: "Agathocles had committed numerous and most varied acts of slaughter during his reign" (Diodorus 21.16.5).
- Diodorus also rejects both of the main sources: Timaeus wrote "lying propaganda against the ruler because of personal enmity" (Diodorus 21.17.1), so "we cannot fairly accept the last five books of this writer's history" (Diodorus 21.17.3); Callias sang "dishonest praises of his paymaster" (Diodorus 21.17.4).
- Polybius attacks Timaeus from the other side and defends the record: "does not this prove that Agathocles had some great and admirable qualities" (Polybius 12.15), and he blames Timaeus' silences: "in history such silence is as mendacious as misstatement" (Polybius 12.15). He also records the worst of Timaeus' abuse, including the claim that in youth Agathocles was "extravagantly addicted to every unnatural vice" (Polybius 12.15).
- Game use: the chronicle at this start can carry two readings of the dead king at once, and the player's own actions decide which one the city believes. Do not resolve it in the data.

**Messana: Diodorus and Polybius differ on who the mercenaries were.** Diodorus simply says that the mercenaries left Syracuse under the settlement (Diodorus 21.18.1). Polybius calls them "some Campanian mercenaries of Agathocles, having for some time cast greedy eyes upon Messene" (Polybius 1.7), so for him the plan predates the expulsion. Diodorus 22.1.2 also calls them Campanians, which agrees with Polybius on origin but not on timing.
