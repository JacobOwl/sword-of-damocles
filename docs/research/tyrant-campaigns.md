## 1. Gelon: from cavalry captain to master of Syracuse (about 491 to 478)

- **He started as a guardsman.** "Gelon, a descendant of the ministering priest Telines, was one of Hippocrates' guard" (Herodotus 7.154.1), then "captain of the entire cavalry" (7.154.2).
- **He took Gela by playing guardian to the heirs.** "Gelon made a pretence of serving the cause of Hippocrates' sons Euclides and Cleandrus, whose rule the citizens would no longer bear. When he had defeated the men of Gela, however, he deposed the sons of Hippocrates and held sway himself" (7.155.1).
- **He took Syracuse by bringing home its exiled landowners.** He "brought back from the town of Casmena to Syracuse both the so-called landed gentry of Syracuse, who had been driven into exile by the common people, and their slaves, the Cyllyrians. He then took possession of that city also, for the Syracusan common people surrendered themselves and it to Gelon at his coming" (7.155.2). Date: 485 in Amendola, earlier in Pausanias (both in `start-480.md`, Disagreements 6).
- **He gave Gela to his brother.** "he took less account of his rule over Gela, which he gave in charge to his brother Hiero; over Syracuse he reigned" (7.156.1).
- **He made Syracuse great by moving whole towns into it.** He did "bring all the people of Camarina to Syracuse and give them its citizenship, razing the township of Camarina, but he did the same thing to more than half of the townsmen of Gela" (7.156.2). The Megarian rich were made citizens; "the common people of Megara ... these too he brought to Syracuse and sold them for slaves to be taken out of Sicily" (7.156.2). Herodotus' reason: "he held the common people to be exceedingly disagreeable to live with" (7.156.3).
- **His reign and death.** "Gelon reigned for seven years" (Diodorus 11.38.7). Dying, "he handed over the kingship to Hieron, his eldest brother" and ordered that the funeral law "should be strictly observed" (11.38.3). The people "accorded Gelon the honours which belong to heroes" (11.38.5).

Game use (invention): Gelon's campaign starts before Syracuse is his. *Move a town's people* is a power a ruler can use anywhere (free mode too), with a ledger: the moved rich become citizens with a grudge or a debt, the moved poor become slaves sold abroad or a faction of the dispossessed.

## 2. Hieron I: the brother who held what Gelon built (about 478 to 467)

- **Length.** Hieron "reigned over the Syracusans eleven years and eight months" (Diodorus 11.38.7); 11.66.4 says "He had ruled eleven years".
- **The popular brother.** Hieron, "observing how popular his brother Polyzelus was among the Syracusans and believing that he was waiting to seize the kingship, was eager to put him out of the way, and so, enlisting foreign soldiers and gathering about his person an organized body of mercenaries" (11.48.3).
- **The command meant to kill.** Sent to relieve Sybaris, Hieron gave the army to Polyzelus "in the belief that he would be slain by the Crotoniates" (11.48.4). Polyzelus refused and "took refuge with Theron, the tyrant of Acragas" (11.48.5).
- **A betrayal that bought peace.** The Himerans offered Hieron their city against Theron; "Hieron, however, having decided to be at peace with Theron, betrayed the Himerans and disclosed to him their secret plans", and Theron "restored Polyzelus to the favour he had previously enjoyed" (11.48.8).
- **Founding a city to be its hero.** "Hieron removed the people of Naxos and Catana from their cities and sent there settlers of his own choosing ... and the name of Catana he changed to Aetna" ... "up to the full sum of ten thousand settlers" (11.49.1). Why: "that he might have a substantial help ready at hand for any need that might arise, but also that from the recently founded state of ten thousand men he might receive the honours accorded to heroes" (11.49.2). At his death he "received the honours which are accorded to heroes, as having been the founder of the city" (11.66.4).
- **The sea war in Italy.** Cumae asked for aid against "the Tyrrhenians, who were at that time masters of the sea", and Hieron "dispatched to their aid a considerable number of triremes" (11.51.1); his fleet "humbled the Tyrrhenians" (11.51.2). The year is the archonship of Acestorides; modern books put it at about 474 (not in the saved texts).
- **War with Acragas.** Against Thrasydaeus, son of Theron: "Greeks were marshalled against Greeks" (11.53.4); the Syracusans "lost some two thousand men against more than four thousand for their opponents" (11.53.5), and Acragas "recovered their democratic form of government" (11.53.5).
- **Meddling abroad.** He set the sons of Anaxilas against their guardian Micythus at Rhegium (11.66.1); Micythus handed everything over and left "accompanied by the goodwill of the populace" (11.66.3).
- **How he ruled, by Diodorus' judgement.** "Hieron ... did not rule over his subjects in the same manner;" ... "for he was avaricious and violent" ... "there were a good many who wished to revolt, but they restrained their inclinations because of Gelon's reputation" (11.67.3 to 11.67.4). Diodorus' verdict, not a fact; Pindar and Bacchylides praise him (see `start-466.md`). Show both.
- **The heir's one year.** "he left the kingdom to his brother Thrasybulus, who ruled over the Syracusans for one year" (11.66.4). Thrasybulus "enlisted a large body of mercenaries" (11.67.5), held "Achradinê, as it is called, and the Island" (11.67.8), lost, and "retired under a truce to Locris" (11.68.4). The city then "maintained its democracy for almost sixty years" (11.68.6).

Game use (invention): Hieron's campaign is the second-generation problem. You hold a borrowed name (Gelon's), a rival brother and a founder's ambition. The sting is the epilogue: the chronicle plays your heir's first year by the engine, and you see whether what you built outlives you.

## 3. Dionysius the younger: the heir (about 367 to 357, and after)

- **His father's death.** After a victory with a tragedy at Athens, Dionysius I "instituted a drinking bout and great feasts" and "fell violently ill from the quantity of liquor he had consumed" (Diodorus 15.74.2).
- **The accession, by assembly.** "Dionysius the younger on his succession to the tyranny first gathered the populace in an assembly and urged them in appropriate words to maintain toward him the loyalty that passed to him with the heritage that he had received from his father" (15.74.5).
- **The half-brothers and the uncle.** Dionysius I had sons by two wives: by the Locrian, "Dionysius, who succeeded to the tyranny", and by the daughter of Hipparinus "two sons Hipparinus and Nysaeus" (16.6.2). Her brother was Dion, "a man who had great proficiency in philosophy" (16.6.3), who "fell under suspicion with the tyrant, for he was considered powerful enough to overthrow the tyranny" (16.6.4).
- **The philosopher at court.** Plato reports Dion's pitch: "the youth of Dionysius, mentioning also how great a desire he had for philosophy and education" (Plato, Letter 7.328). Plato's account of how it went wrong: reports that Dion and Plato were "plotting against him ... caused Dion's expulsion" (7.333). Plato's side of the story; Plutarch and Diodorus differ in detail (`start-357.md`, Disagreements).
- **What he inherited.** Diodorus on Dion's landing: the tyrant "had at his disposal four hundred ships of war, infantry numbering nearly one hundred thousand, ten thousand horse" (16.9.2), and lost because of "the pusillanimity of the tyrant and his subjects' hatred of him" (16.9.3). Diodorus' numbers and judgement; treat the army size as a claim, not a count.

Game use (invention): the heir's campaign is the legitimacy system of `docs/phase3-draft.md` (section 2) with the training wheels off: you start with every object and no reputation of your own.

## 4. Agathocles: the climber who carried the war to Africa (about 317 to 289)

His coup, oath, promises and mercenaries are carded in `start-317.md`; his death and succession in `start-289.md`. Added here:

- **Breaking a blockade to cross.** "Agathocles manned sixty ships" (Diodorus 20.5.1); "the enemy was blockading the port with triremes many times more numerous than his own" (20.5.2). When Carthage's fleet went for incoming grain ships he slipped out, and the grain ships "brought about a great abundance of provisions in Syracuse, when a scarcity of food was already gripping the city" (20.5.4).
- **Burning the ships in Libya.** He told an assembly he had vowed "to Demeter and Corê, the goddesses who protected Sicily ... to offer all the ships as a burnt offering" (20.7.2), and "himself first set out to the trireme of the commander" with a torch (20.7.4). The reason Diodorus gives: "to compel his soldiers in the midst of dangers to have no thought at all of flight" (20.7.5).
- The year of the crossing is given by archon only; modern books say about 310. Not in the saved texts as a BC year.

Game use (invention): a second theatre. Agathocles' campaign needs a small Libyan map (Carthage's farmland and towns) and a home city left under siege. Both are general rules: any ruler with ships can open a second front, and any city can be besieged while its ruler is away (the "absence" idea already built in the commission switch).

## 5. Hieron II: the soldiers' general who became king (about 275 to 264, and on)

- **Elected by the army.** "the military forces of the Syracusans had quarrelled with the citizens, and while stationed near Merganè elected commanders from their own body. These were Artemidorus and Hiero, the latter of whom afterwards became King of Syracuse" (Polybius 1.8). He then "got his political opponents into his hands; but conducted the government with such mildness, and in so lofty a spirit, that the Syracusans, though by no means usually acquiescing in the election of officers by the soldiers, did on this occasion unanimously approve of Hiero as their general" (1.8). Justin: "When Pyrrhus had withdrawn from Sicily, Hieron was made governor of it" (Justin 23.4.1). Date: after Pyrrhus left Sicily; modern ranges run about 275 to 274 (not in the saved texts).
- **A marriage to hold the city while he was away.** He saw that "the despatch of troops, and of magistrates in command of them, was always the signal for revolutionary movements" (1.9). So he married the daughter of Leptines, "by far the most influential man existing" among the common people, "that he might have a representative of his interests left at home" (1.9).
- **Spending the old mercenaries.** Seeing the old mercenaries "disaffected and mutinous", he led them against the Mamertines and "the mercenaries he thrust to the front and allowed them to be completely cut to pieces by the foreigners" (1.9). Then, "after enlisting on his own account a sufficient body of mercenaries, he thenceforth carried on the business of the government in security" (1.9).
- **The victory that made him king.** He "fully armed and energetically drilled the citizen levies" and "engaged the enemy on the Mylaean plain near the River Longanus", "took their leaders prisoners" and "was himself greeted by all the allies with the title of King" (1.9). Diodorus' fragments tell a fuller campaign: he "took Mylae by storm and acquired fifteen hundred soldiers" (Diodorus 22.13.1) and "drove the Mamertines into a narrow area" (22.13.2). The date of Longanus is disputed by modern historians (about 269 or about 265); not in the saved texts.
- **Cheated of Messana by Carthage.** The Carthaginian Hannibal at Lipara came "ostensibly to offer his congratulations, but in reality seeking to outmanoeuvre Hiero by deceit", turned to Messana and dissuaded the Mamertines from surrender (22.13.7). "Hiero, outwitted by the Phoenician, abandoned the siege" (22.13.8).
- **Rome arrives.** Some Mamertines turned to Carthage, "others set about sending an embassy to Rome" (Polybius 1.10). Hiero "made a treaty with the Carthaginians" to expel the Mamertines (1.11). When the consul crossed, "Hiero, thinking that the Carthaginians had treacherously permitted the crossing, fled to Syracuse" (Diodorus 23.3.1).
- **The switch of sides.** Seeing "the number and crushing strength of the legions of Rome", Hiero "began sending messages to the Consuls, proposing peace and friendship with them" (Polybius 1.16). Rome accepted, "their chief motive being the consideration of provisions" (1.16).
- **The long reign.** Polybius: "Hiero gained the sovereignty of Syracuse and her allies by his own unaided abilities without inheriting wealth, or reputation" and was "established king of Syracuse without putting to death, banishing, or harassing any one of the citizens" ... "during a reign of fifty-four years he preserved peace for the country" (Polybius 7.8). Polybius' judgement, and it sits oddly beside 1.9's sacrificed mercenaries; show both.

Game use (invention): Hieron II's campaign is the end of the game's span. It starts with the army's election and ends at Rome's crossing (264), the span's last year (`docs/phase3-draft.md`, section 7). Its final choice is the one Polybius describes: Carthage or Rome.
