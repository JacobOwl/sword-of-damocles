## Dates

The window runs from the revolt against Thrasybulus to the island-wide settlement: about 466 to 461 BC.

Diodorus dates by Athenian archons, so the window is five or six of his years:

- Hieron dies and Thrasybulus takes over, in the year of the archon Lysistratus: "When Lysistratus was archon in Athens" (Diodorus 11.66.1), "he left the kingdom to his brother Thrasybulus" (Diodorus 11.66.4).
- Thrasybulus is thrown out in the next archon year, Lysanias: "When Lysanias was archon in Athens" (Diodorus 11.67.1), "Thrasybulus, the king of the Syracusans, was driven from his throne" (Diodorus 11.67.1).
- The mercenaries' revolt starts in the same Diodorus year as the vote on offices (Diodorus 11.72.3 to 11.73.1) and is still being fought two archon years later, under Euthippus (Diodorus 11.75.1, then 11.76.1).
- The island-wide settlement with the mercenaries comes at the end of that stretch: "practically all the cities, being eager to make an end of the wars, came to a common decision" (Diodorus 11.76.5).
- Modern dates for the same events. Amendola: "account of the events following the Syracusan revolt against Thrasybulus in" (Amendola, section 2), and the next line of his text reads "466/5 and the subsequent collapse of Deinomenid rule, which ultimately led to" (Amendola, section 2). He dates the offices decree to 463: "After a decree was enacted in 463 stipulating that" (Amendola, section 2). He dates the common agreement to 461: "enacted by most Sicilian poleis in 461 (Syracuse," (Amendola, section 2).
- How long Hieron and Thrasybulus ruled is disputed. See Disagreements.
- Later trouble in the same democracy, which the game can reach by playing on: Tyndarides and the law of petalism come about ten Diodorus years later, under the archon Ariston (Diodorus 11.86.1 to 11.87.6).

So: a six-season window, with the citadel changing hands twice inside it.

## The six power objects

1. Citadel (Ortygia the Island, and Achradina)
   - At the very start the ruler holds both: "seizing Achradinê, as it is called, and the Island, which was fortified" (Diodorus 11.67.8). The citizens hold the rest: "The Syracusans at the outset seized a part of the city which is called Tychê" (Diodorus 11.68.1).
   - When the tyrant leaves, the same two quarters pass to the new citizens: "they seized in the city both Achradinê and the Island" (Diodorus 11.73.1), described as "both these places having their own well-built fortifications" (Diodorus 11.73.1).
   - The citizens answer with a wall: "that part of it which faced Epipolae they blocked off by a wall" (Diodorus 11.73.2). The wall is a siege weapon: the holders of the citadel "were in want of equipment and short of food" (Diodorus 11.73.3).
   - For the game this is the cleanest power object in the whole period. Whoever holds Ortygia cannot be stormed, and cannot eat.
2. Treasury
   - Under the tyrant it is a royal chest filled by confiscation: "confiscating their possessions into the royal treasury" (Diodorus 11.67.5).
   - Under the democracy it is spent by vote, on cult and on soldiers' prizes: "to sacrifice to the gods, in connection with the games, four hundred and fifty bulls" (Diodorus 11.72.2) and "giving them each a mina of silver" (Diodorus 11.76.2), paid to "the elite troops, six hundred in number" (Diodorus 11.76.2).
   - It also pays for a statue: "to make a colossal statue of Zeus the Liberator" (Diodorus 11.72.2).
   - Proposal (invention): the treasury has two meters, cash and credit with the citizens. Confiscation fills the first and empties the second.
3. Mint
   - Not in the saved texts for these years. No ancient line in these files says who struck Syracusan coin after 466.
   - Coin evidence, with the museum's own dating: the American Numismatic Society catalogue lists a "Silver Tetradrachm, Syracuse, 466 BCE–450 BCE. 1997.9.30" (ANS MANTIS tetradrachm list) whose reverse is described as "ΣΥΡΑΚΟΣΙΟΝ: Arethusa head l., four dolphins around" (ANS MANTIS tetradrachm list). The legend is the city's name, not a man's. For the earlier tyrant years the same catalogue has pieces dated "474 BCE - 470 BCE" (ANS MANTIS tetradrachm list) with the same city legend. So the coins of both regimes name Syracuse. The catalogue does not say who controlled the dies.
   - Proposal (invention): the mint is held by the assembly through an annual board. A tyrant who takes the city takes the mint but keeps the city's name on the coin, because taking his own name off it is worth loyalty.
4. Fleet
   - The ruler starts with it and loses it: "Thrasybulus sailed forth with his ships against the enemy" (Diodorus 11.68.3), "after suffering defeat in the battle with the loss of numerous triremes" (Diodorus 11.68.3).
   - The allies supply the citizens' fleet: "others warships fully equipped for action" (Diodorus 11.68.2).
   - The citizens then beat the mercenaries at sea too: "they defeated the rebels in a sea-battle" (Diodorus 11.76.1).
   - Under the democracy the fleet is commanded by an elected admiral who can be sacked: "the Syracusans chose Phaÿllus as admiral" (Diodorus 11.88.4), and later "choosing another general, Apelles, they dispatched him with sixty triremes against the Tyrrhenians" (Diodorus 11.88.5). Sixty triremes is the one hard fleet number in the saved texts for this democracy, and it is a few years after the window.
5. Generalship
   - Under the tyrant, held in his own person with hired troops: "he enlisted a large body of mercenaries, preparing in this way a legion with which to oppose the citizen soldiery" (Diodorus 11.67.5).
   - In the revolt, the citizens elect leaders: "choosing men who would take the lead, set about as one man to destroy the tyranny" (Diodorus 11.67.6), and "once they had been organized by their leaders they clung stubbornly to their freedom" (Diodorus 11.67.6). None of these leaders is named in the saved texts.
   - Under the democracy the generalship is an office with a death penalty attached: "they appointed a new general, to whom they assigned a strong army" (Diodorus 11.91.2); Bolcon was found "guilty of treason and put him to death" (Diodorus 11.91.2); Phaÿllus was found guilty of treachery: "The Syracusans found him guilty of treachery and exiled him" (Diodorus 11.88.5).
6. Assembly
   - The real holder of power after the tyrant goes. It votes the constitution: "they held a meeting of the Assembly, and after deliberating on forming a democracy of their own they all voted unanimously" (Diodorus 11.72.2).
   - It votes who may hold office: "As for all the magistracies, they proposed to assign them to the original citizens" (Diodorus 11.72.3).
   - It is summoned by magistrates: "the magistrates called a meeting of the Assembly and laid before it the question of what should be done with Ducetius" (Diodorus 11.92.2).
   - It can be swung in one meeting: "The people thereupon cried out as with one voice from every side to spare the suppliant" (Diodorus 11.92.4).
   - It later invents a tool against strong men: "the name of the most influential citizen had to be written on an olive leaf" (Diodorus 11.87.1), and the loser "had to go into exile for five years" (Diodorus 11.87.1). Diodorus names it: "the Syracusans used the name petalism" (Diodorus 11.87.3).
   - Proposal (invention): petalism is a card the assembly can enact and repeal, as it did: "the Syracusans changed their minds and repealed the law of petalism" (Diodorus 11.87.6).

## The factions

1. The old citizens. They take the offices: "they proposed to assign them to the original citizens" (Diodorus 11.72.3). Modern reading of the decree: Amendola says the rule meant that "only the old citizens were eligible to hold magistracies (τὰς δὲ ἀρχὰς ἁπάσας" (Amendola, section 2). Their descent from the old landowning order is in Herodotus for the earlier generation, which names "the so-called landed gentry of Syracuse, who had been driven into exile by the common people" (Herodotus 7.155.2).
2. The new citizens, that is the mercenaries Gelon enrolled. "Gelon had enrolled as citizens more than ten thousand foreign mercenaries" (Diodorus 11.72.3), and of those "there were left at the time in question more than seven thousand" (Diodorus 11.72.3). Their grievance: "These aliens resented their being excluded from the dignity attending magistracies" (Diodorus 11.73.1). The reason given for excluding them: "they were suspicious lest men who had been brought up in the way of tyranny" (Diodorus 11.72.3) and "had served in war under a monarch might attempt a revolution" (Diodorus 11.72.3). Diodorus then adds, bleakly: "And that is what actually happened" (Diodorus 11.72.3).
   - They are better soldiers than the citizens: "in number the mercenaries were inferior to the Syracusans, yet in experience of warfare they were far superior" (Diodorus 11.73.3).
   - Aristotle makes the same point as a general law: "the Syracusans after the period of the tyrants conferred citizenship on their foreign troops and mercenaries and then faction set in" (Aristotle, Politics 1303b).
   - Amendola notes that Aristotle keeps two groups apart where Diodorus blurs them: the transplanted Greeks and "the thousands of mercenaries made citizens of Syracuse" (Amendola, section 2).
3. The poor citizens, as a following for ambitious men: "began by gathering about him many of the poor, and organizing them into an armed unit" (Diodorus 11.86.4).
4. The respectable citizens as a counter-force: "the most respectable citizens, who had organized themselves, seized the revolutionists and put them to death" (Diodorus 11.86.5).
5. The demagogues, a named faction type in Diodorus: "a multitude of demagogues and sycophants was arising" (Diodorus 11.87.5), with "the youth were cultivating cleverness in oratory" (Diodorus 11.87.5).
6. The elder citizens, as a moderating bloc: "the more fairminded of the elder citizens came forward" (Diodorus 11.92.3). Against them: "Some of those who were accustomed to curry favour with the people" (Diodorus 11.92.3).
7. The ruler's household and its hangers-on. The settlers Hieron planted are the tyrant's reserve: "the colonists whom Hieron had settled in Catana and his other allies" (Diodorus 11.67.7).
8. The Siceli of the interior, an outside faction that can be an ally or an invader: "Ducetius, the leader of the Siceli" (Diodorus 11.76.3). They are called in as allies in the revolt: "the cities of the Siceli in the interior of the island" (Diodorus 11.68.1).

## Carthage's posture

Quiet, and absent from the saved texts for these years. This is the opposite of 480.

- The settled state of the island before the window: "an almost complete peace pervaded the island, the Carthaginians having finally been humbled" (Diodorus 11.38.1).
- No Carthaginian army appears in Diodorus 11.66 to 11.92. What appears instead is a quarrel between two cities of the far west: "In Sicily a war arose between the peoples of Egesta and Lilybaeum over the land on the Mazarus River" (Diodorus 11.86.2), fought so hard that "both cities lost heavily but did not slacken their rivalry" (Diodorus 11.86.2).
- Carthaginian and Phoenician settlement in the west is a standing fact: "the city Motye, inhabited by Libyans and Phoenicians" (Pausanias 5.25.5). Pausanias also says of the Phoenicians and Libyans of Sicily that they "are settlers from Carthage" (Pausanias 5.25.6).
- The return is far off in Diodorus's own forward look: Himera's people lived in peace "for fifty-eight years; but at the expiration of this period the city was conquered and razed to the ground by the Carthaginians" (Diodorus 11.49.4).
- Proposal (invention): Carthage is a dormant pressure with a long fuse. In the game it watches, trades and lends, and its interest rises when a Greek city asks it in. A Greek asking Carthage in is exactly what happened before Himera, where the invasion came "mainly through the efforts of Anaxilaus son of Cretines" (Herodotus 7.165.1).

## The island

- Syracuse: free, and then at war with its own garrison. "The Syracusans, having liberated their native city in this manner, gave permission to the mercenaries to withdraw from Syracuse" (Diodorus 11.68.5). Then the same men seize the citadel (Diodorus 11.73.1).
- Gela, Acragas, Selinus, Himera: free allies of the revolt. "they dispatched ambassadors to Gela, Acragas, and Selinus, and also to Himera" (Diodorus 11.68.1), and "all these cities acceded to this request eagerly" (Diodorus 11.68.2). Acragas had become a democracy a few years earlier: "the Acragantini, having now recovered their democratic form of government" (Diodorus 11.53.5).
- Syracuse's policy toward the rest: "which were either in the hands of tyrants or had garrisons" (Diodorus 11.68.5), and the same sentence has them "established democracies in them" (Diodorus 11.68.5).
- Catana, renamed Aetna by Hieron: held by Hieron's ten thousand settlers, then lost. The planting: "the name of Catana he changed to Aetna" (Diodorus 11.49.1), with "five thousand from the Peloponnesus and added an equal number of others from Syracuse" (Diodorus 11.49.1). The loss: "were defeated in a number of engagements and were expelled from Catana" (Diodorus 11.76.3), after which "the original inhabitants of Catana, after a long period, got back their native city" (Diodorus 11.76.3).
- Rhegium and Zancle across the strait: the tyrant's sons thrown out. "Rhegians along with Zanclians expelled the sons of Anaxilas, who were ruling over them, and liberated their fatherlands" (Diodorus 11.76.5).
- Camarina: refounded by Gela. "Geloans, who had been the original settlers of Camarina, portioned that land out in allotments" (Diodorus 11.76.5).
- Messene (Messenia in this translation): the dumping ground for the mercenaries. They were given "permission to take with them their own goods and to settle one and all in Messenia" (Diodorus 11.76.5). Amendola calls the place Zancle-Messana and says the agreement allowed "the mercenaries still in service, originally" (Amendola, section 2) to resettle there.
- The Sicel interior: consolidating into one league. Ducetius "gathered all the cities which were of the same race, with the exception of Hybla, into one and a common federation" (Diodorus 11.88.6), and built "an important city, which he named Palicê" (Diodorus 11.88.6).
- Egesta and Lilybaeum in the west: at war with each other (Diodorus 11.86.2), quoted above.
- Exiles coming home everywhere: "the peoples who had been expelled from their own cities while Hieron was king" (Diodorus 11.76.4) "returned to their fatherlands and expelled from their cities the men who had wrongfully seized for themselves the habitations of others" (Diodorus 11.76.4).
- The general land settlement: the cities "with almost no exceptions portioned out their lands in allotments among all their citizens" (Diodorus 11.76.6).

## About 15 people

Real people (history):

1. Thrasybulus, brother of Hieron, ruler at the start of the window. "being a violent man and murderous by nature, he put to death many citizens unjustly" (Diodorus 11.67.5). He ends in exile: "retired under a truce to Locris" (Diodorus 11.68.4), and "spent the rest of his life there in private station" (Diodorus 11.68.7).
2. Hieron, just dead when the window opens. "Hieron, the king of the Syracusans, died in Catana and received the honours which are accorded to heroes" (Diodorus 11.66.4). His character, in Diodorus: "he was avaricious and violent" (Diodorus 11.67.4). In verse while alive: "Fortunate in your fate, commander of the Syracusans" (Bacchylides 5).
3. The son of Gelon, unnamed in the saved texts, the man Thrasybulus corrupted. Aristotle: "Thrasybulus the brother of Hiero paid court to the son of Gelo and urged him into indulgences in order that he himself might rule" (Aristotle, Politics 1312b). The counter-plot: "the son’s connections banded together a body of confederates" (Aristotle, Politics 1312b).
4. Deinomenes, son of Hieron, praised at Aetna. Pindar: "beside Deinomenes sing loud praises for the reward of the four-horse chariot" (Pindar, Pythian 1), and the ode addresses "the king of Aetna, for whom Hieron founded that city with god-built freedom" (Pindar, Pythian 1). The saved text does not say in so many words that Deinomenes is that king, and the ode is from before the window.
5. Polyzelus, brother of Gelon and Hieron, the popular one. "observing how popular his brother Polyzelus was among the Syracusans" (Diodorus 11.48.3). Whether he is alive in 466 is not in the saved texts.
6. Ducetius, leader of the Siceli, outside player and later suppliant. "Ducetius, the leader of the Siceli, harbouring a grudge against the inhabitants of Catana" (Diodorus 11.76.3). His surrender years later: "seating himself at the altars he became a suppliant of the city" (Diodorus 11.92.1).
7. Tyndarides, would-be tyrant of the new democracy. "a man by the name of Tyndarides, a rash fellow full of effrontery" (Diodorus 11.86.4). He is "brought to trial and condemned to death" (Diodorus 11.86.4). He belongs to a year about ten Diodorus years after the window, and shows what the start can grow into.
8. Phaÿllus, elected admiral who took a bribe. "he secretly accepted a bribe of money from the Tyrrhenians" (Diodorus 11.88.4).
9. Apelles, his replacement. "choosing another general, Apelles" (Diodorus 11.88.5).
10. Bolcon, general executed for treason. "the Syracusans found their general Bolcon, who was responsible for the defeat and was thought to have had secret dealings with Ducetius" (Diodorus 11.91.2).
11. Micythus, the honest steward of Rhegium, a model of the man who gives power back. "Micythus, who was an upright man, gathered together the old family friends of the children and rendered so honest an accounting" (Diodorus 11.66.2). He refuses to take it up again: "Micythus, however, did not accede to the request" (Diodorus 11.66.3). Pausanias adds that he had been a slave: "became his slave and steward of his property" (Pausanias 5.26.4).
12. The sons of Anaxilas, the tyrants over the strait. Hieron set them up: "summoning to Syracuse the sons of Anaxilas, the former tyrant of Zanclê, and giving them great gifts" (Diodorus 11.66.1). They are expelled inside the window (Diodorus 11.76.5).
13. Corax, named only in the modern article, from a tradition going back to Aristotle through Cicero: "Corax, a former partisan of Hieron and a leading figure in the events of 466" (Amendola, note 67). The same note has forensic oratory beginning at Syracuse from "disputes arising out of the full reintegration of the" old citizens "through the activity of" that man (Amendola, note 67). Treat him as a figure from scholarship, not from a saved ancient text.
14. Hagesias of Stymphalos, Hieron's seer. Amendola: he "served as seer to Hieron on military campaigns and at city" (Amendola, section 2) foundations, and was killed "before being executed after the fall of the Deinomenids in 466" (Amendola, section 2). His text breaks the sentence across two lines, so the two fragments are quoted separately. Again, modern scholarship citing a scholion, not a saved ancient text.
15. Phormis of Maenalus, an old Arcadian soldier of both tyrants who was still rich. His own inscription: "Phormis dedicated me, An Arcadian of Maenalus, now of Syracuse" (Pausanias 5.27.2). He had served "in the campaigns of Gelon and afterwards of his brother Hieron" (Pausanias 5.27.1). Whether he is alive in 466 is not in the saved texts, but his type is exactly the man the offices decree shuts out.
16. Lycortas of Syracuse, his friend and fellow dedicator: "he who dedicated the offerings was Lycortas of Syracuse" (Pausanias 5.27.7).

Invented people (invention). Names are taken from Greek names that appear in the saved texts, but these are not the real men who bear them:

17. Acestorides, captain of the seven thousand in the citadel. Why: Diodorus never names a single leader of the new citizens, and the player needs a face on the other side of the wall. Name borrowed from an archon in Diodorus 11.51.1.
18. Praxigerus, an old citizen suing to get his grandfather's land back. Why: Amendola's note 67 describes exactly these property trials but no litigant is named. Name borrowed from an archon in Diodorus 11.54.1.
19. Sosistratus, a rower and ward leader of the poor in Tyche. Why: Diodorus names the poor as a bloc (Diodorus 11.86.4) and never a person in it. Name borrowed from an archon in Diodorus 11.85.1.
20. Theageneides, priest of Zeus the Liberator, keeper of the new festival. Why: the assembly voted the cult and the games (Diodorus 11.72.2), so the cult needs a living officeholder. Name borrowed from an archon in Diodorus 11.65.1.
21. Antidotus, an olive-leaf collector, the clerk who runs a petalism vote. Why: the law is described but not its officers (Diodorus 11.87.1). Name borrowed from an archon in Diodorus 11.91.1.

## What the player starts as

Default: Thrasybulus, who holds Syracuse, meaning Achradina and the Island, with the mercenaries and the Aetna settlers. Diodorus gives his force: "his army numbered all told almost fifteen thousand men" (Diodorus 11.67.7). The city outside the walls is already in arms: "the whole city was in arms against him" (Diodorus 11.67.7). He tries talk first: "he at first attempted to stop the revolt by persuasion" (Diodorus 11.67.7). History gave him ten months or a year, depending on the source. The game asks whether a man in that position can last longer, and what he must give up to do it.

Two other playable starts:

1. A leader of the free Syracusans. Grounded in "choosing men who would take the lead" (Diodorus 11.67.6). The player begins with Tyche, no fleet, no treasury, and must bring in allied cities by embassy: "dispatched ambassadors to Gela, Acragas, and Selinus" (Diodorus 11.68.1). The win condition is not killing the tyrant but what comes next: whether the player writes the offices rule narrow, as history did, or wide, and what that costs. The real danger is at the end, not the start, since the man who liberates can be the next Tyndarides.
2. A captain of the new citizens, a mercenary made Syracusan by Gelon. Grounded in "more than seven thousand" (Diodorus 11.72.3) and in their grievance (Diodorus 11.73.1). The player has the best soldiers on the island, a fortified quarter, no farmland and no vote. Every path is a bad one: hold out and starve, go to Messene, or find a Syracusan patron. Aristotle's rule hangs over it: "a king’s body-guard consists of citizens, a tyrant’s of foreign mercenaries" (Aristotle, Politics 1311a).

An optional fourth, for a slower game (invention): Praxigerus the returning claimant, who starts with a lawsuit instead of an army.

## Stories this start can make

Each of these is a pressure, not a script.

1. The citadel that cannot be stormed and cannot eat. Diodorus gives both halves: the quarters are strong, "both these places having their own well-built fortifications" (Diodorus 11.73.1), and the holders "were in want of equipment and short of food" (Diodorus 11.73.3). The simulation should be able to produce sieges that end in a bargain, not an assault.
2. The vote that makes the next war. The assembly excludes the new citizens (Diodorus 11.72.3), and Diodorus writes the consequence in four words: "And that is what actually happened" (Diodorus 11.72.3). The game should let a legal decision, taken in one season by a majority with a reason, be the cause of the next civil war.
3. The liberator who becomes the danger. The city beats one tyrant, then meets Tyndarides, who built "a personal bodyguard ready for an attempt to set up a tyranny" (Diodorus 11.86.4), and answers with petalism, under which "the most influential men were being sent into exile" (Diodorus 11.87.4) while "it was the basest citizens and such as excelled in effrontery who were giving their attention to public affairs" (Diodorus 11.87.4). A free city that keeps producing tyrants is the whole game in one loop.
4. The general on trial. Two generals in a few years: one exiled for a bribe (Diodorus 11.88.5), one killed on suspicion, "thought to have had secret dealings with Ducetius" (Diodorus 11.91.2). The simulation should let a victory be undone by a rumour, and let the player be the rumour.
5. The suppliant at the altar. Ducetius walks into the market place at night and sits at the altars (Diodorus 11.92.1), and the assembly spares him. The system should be able to produce mercy that is also a calculation, with speakers on both sides: the flatterers "advised that they should punish him as an enemy" (Diodorus 11.92.3) and the elders argued "to slay the victim of Fortune was not fitting" (Diodorus 11.92.3).

## Disagreements

1. How long Hieron ruled. Diodorus, in one place: "reigned over the Syracusans eleven years and eight months" (Diodorus 11.38.7). Diodorus, in another: "He had ruled eleven years" (Diodorus 11.66.4). Aristotle: "Hiero ruled ten years" (Aristotle, Politics 1315b). Three numbers, none chosen here.
2. How long Thrasybulus ruled. Diodorus: "ruled over the Syracusans for one year" (Diodorus 11.66.4). Aristotle: "Thrasybulus was expelled after ten months" (Aristotle, Politics 1315b).
3. Why the Deinomenids fell. Diodorus blames the man's own cruelty: "he kept incurring more and most the hatred of the citizens by outraging many and executing others, he compelled the victims to revolt" (Diodorus 11.67.6). The odd phrase in the middle is the saved Loeb text as scraped, not a change of mine. Aristotle blames the family: the tyranny fell "when the partners in it fall into discord, as the tyranny of the family of Gelo was destroyed" (Aristotle, Politics 1312b). In Aristotle's version the plotters meant to remove one man only, "in order that the tyranny might not be put down entirely but only Thrasybulus" (Aristotle, Politics 1312b), and lost control: "their confederates seizing the opportunity expelled them all" (Aristotle, Politics 1312b). Diodorus's own account has no such insider plot. The game should model both routes.
4. Whether the democracy was stable. Diodorus, summing up: "it maintained its democracy for almost sixty years" (Diodorus 11.68.6), and "From this time the city enjoyed peace and increased greatly in prosperity" (Diodorus 11.68.6). Diodorus, a few chapters later: "the cities were in an unhealthy state and falling back again into civil strife and disorders" (Diodorus 11.86.3), and "it was especially in Syracuse that this malady prevailed" (Diodorus 11.86.3). The same author on the same subject.
5. The numbers. The tyrant's force: "almost fifteen thousand men" (Diodorus 11.67.7). The surviving new citizens: "more than seven thousand" (Diodorus 11.72.3). Amendola passes on a scholar's doubt about the survival rate in his note 43, written in Italian, where Bettalli calls it a strangely high percentage. I am not quoting the Italian as an English fact. Treat the figures as ranges.
6. Who exactly was excluded. Diodorus speaks loosely of aliens enfranchised under Gelon (Diodorus 11.72.3). Amendola reads Aristotle as keeping two groups apart, the transplanted Greeks and "the thousands of mercenaries made citizens of Syracuse" (Amendola, section 2), and says that the picture "offered by Diodorus, instead, is less straightforward" (Amendola, section 2).
7. Whether the new citizens kept their citizenship. Amendola says they "were excluded from office, despite retaining their civic status.43 This situation" (Amendola, section 2), that is, citizens without offices. Diodorus does not spell this out. The distinction matters for the game, because it decides whether the mercenaries are rebels or citizens in arms.
8. Dating. Diodorus packs the fall and the start of the mercenary war into one archon year. Amendola spreads the same events over 466/5, 463 and 461. Both are shown. The game should let a window cover several seasons rather than pick one year.
9. Where the land went. Diodorus says the cities "portioned out their lands in allotments among all their citizens" (Diodorus 11.76.6). Amendola reports a modern argument, Asheri's, that at Syracuse the redistribution was really a reshuffle of city quarters, with the old citizens restored to "their former holdings in Ortygia and Achradina as a result of such ownership" disputes (Amendola, section 2). That is a scholar's reconstruction, not a line of Diodorus.

## Not found in the saved texts

- The names of the Syracusan leaders of the revolt. Diodorus says leaders were chosen and never names one.
- The name of any leader of the seven thousand new citizens.
- The constitution itself: how many magistrates, what they were called, how the assembly was summoned, who could speak, whether there was a council.
- Who ran the mint, and whose authority the city's coins carry in these years.
- Any Carthaginian move between 466 and 461.
- The fate of Gelon's son, of Polyzelus, or of Damarete after 466.
- What happened to the mercenaries' land and houses inside the walls, except through modern reconstruction.
- Numbers for the Syracusan citizen body, old or new, beyond the ten thousand and seven thousand figures.
- The terms of the truce that let Thrasybulus go to Locris, beyond the word truce.
- Whether Deinomenes son of Hieron was still ruling anything in 466.
- Grain, taxes and rents: nothing in the saved texts for these years, which the people and world layers of the game would need.
