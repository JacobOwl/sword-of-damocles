## In five lines

1. **Your town idea is the best of the three, and the best sourced.** Armies in these wars were bundles of town levies, mercenary bands and peoples who left, broke or changed sides when their home turned against you. Diodorus gives at least ten cases in 406 to 393 alone.
2. **So an army becomes a short list of named contingents** ("Gela's levy", "the guard", "the horsemen", "Campanians from Aetna"), each with a heart (steady, wavering, ready to leave) read from its town or faction. Towns matter in battle, and battle news matters in towns.
3. **Arms (horse, light troops, chariots) ride on the contingents as a tag**, and matter mostly through the ground. No rock-paper-scissors table, no army builder.
4. **Before each battle the generals contest the ground.** The better general, knowing the land, and a lucky roll picks where to fight: plain, hills, river, pass, marsh or shore. The ground then favours some arms and hurts others.
5. **No elephants in 405 to 395.** The first in Sicily in the saved texts are Pyrrhus' (about 278); Carthage's first appear in 262, after the game's last year. They belong only in the 289 campaign, as Pyrrhus' army.

## What exists now

An army is one number (strength points of about 5,000 men) plus how many of those are the guard. The muster already knows which towns sent men ("1 from Gela"), then forgets it. A battle is a d6 each plus modifiers (strength, general's traits, own ground, hunger, the guard's mood, you in person), and with the battle plate a plan, a reserve and one earned stratagem. Places have a few tags (`port`, `marsh`, `straits`). Carthage's army is also one number.

## A. Contingents (Jacob's idea)

**What the player sees.** On the army card and the battle plate, the army is three to seven stamped tokens instead of one number:

> Your army, 25,000 men: the citizens' levy (10,000) · the guard (5,000, Lacedaemonian and Campanian) · Gela's levy (5,000, *wavering*) · the horsemen (1 token, *wavering*)

Each token is one strength point or more, from one home: a town, a faction (citizens, guard), or a people (Sicels, Campanians). Its **heart** has three marks and is read, never rolled:

- **Steady**: its town leans to you, or its faction is content.
- **Wavering**: its town leans away, its faction is angry, or it is hungry or unpaid.
- **Ready to leave**: its town leans to Carthage, the town board's leading house is Carthage's friend, or its people were wronged (sacked kin, a broken oath).

The reason is always shown in a plain line: "Gela's men waver: Gela's council fears Carthage's army at Acragas."

**What hearts do (one rule each, all shown before the battle):**

1. **A wavering contingent counts one less** (−1 to the army if any token wavers; never more than −2 in all).
2. **The turning moment.** In a battle you lose, or draw, the worst-hearted contingent breaks first and takes the heaviest losses, and its town hears of it (the town-board news rule, already built). In a battle you win, a wavering contingent that held turns steady, and its town's lean rises one. History: Italian Greeks and horsemen at Gela (13.112.3); Leptines' wing at Cronium, a later pattern (15.17.1-2).
3. **Ready to leave = leaves.** At the start of a season, one contingent that is ready to leave goes home (Sicilian Greeks, 14.61.3, already the drift-home rule, now naming who) or, if its town leans to Carthage and Carthage's army is within reach, **goes over** and joins Carthage's army (the Sicels, 14.58.1). A warning line comes a season before: "The Sicels in your camp are talking with Himilcon's envoys."
4. **Hostages hold it.** A new order, *Take hostages* from an allied town (costs that town's lean 1): its contingent cannot leave or go over while you hold them (the Campanians of Aetna, 14.61.6). Killing hostages is a later petition, not part of this.

**Carthage the same way.** Carthage's army gets its own tokens: Libyans, Iberians, Campanians, Sicels or Sicilian allies, the citizen Carthaginians (rarely). Their hearts read Carthage's blocs (the Libyan subjects' mood for the Libyans), pay (Carthage's silver for mercenaries), and for Sicels the Sicel towns. So the player gets **levers on Carthage's army**: win over the Sicels and Carthage's left melts away (14.75.6); keep Carthage short of silver and its Campanians mutiny (13.88.2); beat the army and the Iberians may take your pay (14.75.8-9, a new outcome: "hire the beaten mercenaries").

**What it reuses.** The census houses (town boards) for town hearts; faction moods for the citizens' levy and guard; the drift-home rule; the "send the distrusted men in first" stratagem now names *which* contingent; losses already split between guard and levy.

**Cost.** Medium. The army goes from a number to a list (save files, losses, reinforce, retreat, Carthage's army, the bots). The display is cheap once that exists.

## B. Arms (unit types), kept few

One tag per contingent. Five arms in 405 to 395, from the sources:

| Arm | Who | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy foot | citizens, Greek town levies, Libyans, the guard | the body of the army; no special rule |
| Horse | the Syracusan horsemen; Carthage's cavalry | +1 on a plain; after a won battle the loser loses more (pursuit); an army with horse cannot be surprised by a night march, and its foragers are safe (13.88.1, 13.109.3) |
| Light troops | Sicels, Campanian and Iberian skirmishers, slingers | +1 in hills or a pass; deny the enemy's foraging in that area (13.109.3) |
| Chariots | Carthage only (400 by Ephorus' count, 14.54.5) | +1 on a plain, −2 at a river or in broken ground (Crimisus, 339, 16.80.2: a later pattern) |
| Engines | built at Syracuse after the catapult (14.42.1) | sieges only: shorten a siege of a walled town; slow to move (half a day's march less) |

**The political twist (cheap, strong):** **the horsemen are the rich families.** In 405 they revolted and tried to kill Dionysius on the road (13.112.3); in 396 their spokesman Theodorus stood up for liberty (14.64.5). So the horse token's heart is the rich families' mood. You want horse for the plains, but taking them to war puts your enemies under arms and away from home at once, a real dilemma the game already half has with the guard.

**Not recommended:** a CK3 or Imperator counter table (spearmen beat horse beat archers). It adds a choice the player must look up, and Jacob read only the structured parts of the screen.

**Cost.** Cheap on top of A: one tag and a few modifiers. Engines are a small separate change to sieges.

## C. Ground (Jacob's "set up good terrain")

**Data.** Each place gets two or three ground choices, as tags in `data/island.json`: e.g. Syracuse *marsh, shore, hills (Epipolae)*; Gela *river, plain, shore*; Acragas *hills, river*; Leontini *plain, river*; Motya *shore*; Aetna *hills, pass*. The tags are invention unless a passage names the ground (Gela's river, 13.109.5; Syracuse's marsh, 14.70.5). Meeting on the road between towns uses the ground of the place where they meet.

**Contesting the field.** Before the roll, each general rolls one die for the ground:

> d6 + the general's skill (traits, already in the game, capped at 2) + 1 if fighting in friendly land or with a local ally (Agyris' men "familiar with the territory", 14.96.1) + 1 if he has horse (scouts)

The higher roll picks the ground from the place's choices. **Win it by 3 or more and you also catch them badly placed** (crossing a river, strung out on the march): +1 more. So a better general usually picks, and luck sometimes overturns him, which is exactly Jacob's line.

**What the player chooses.** Not a pick inside the battle (a battle Carthage starts is fought in the world step, before the player acts; thread 46 noted this). Instead a **standing wish** set with the battle plan, one of three:
- *Seek open ground* (good if you have more horse, or more men),
- *Seek broken ground* (good for light troops and the smaller army),
- *Keep to strong places* (you defend a hill or a river line: the enemy must attack you; +1 to you, but if the enemy wins the ground roll he may simply refuse and ravage instead).

If you win the ground roll, the game uses your wish; if Carthage wins, its general's habit picks (bold generals seek the plain, careful ones the hills). One line explains: "Himilcon outmarched you and chose the plain by the river at Gela. His chariots like it; your Sicels do not."

**What ground does (small, readable):**
- Plain: horse +1, chariots +1, light troops 0.
- Hills or a pass: light troops +1, the defender +1, chariots −2, horse 0.
- River: the side that must cross it −1 (whoever lost the ground roll); chariots −2.
- Marsh: no horse bonus; the camp there sickens (the plague rule, already in).
- Shore: your ships stratagem applies (already in), and a beaten army that reaches its ships loses less.

**Refusing battle.** If you lose the ground roll by 3 or more you may refuse (fall back one stop) instead of fighting. It costs what it cost Dionysius twice: the allied contingents waver and may go home (14.61.3), and the citizens' levy angers (14.96.2).

**Cost.** Small to medium. Tags, one roll, one wish on the plate, a few modifiers. Moving Carthage-started battles into the player step is not needed with the standing wish.

## How other games do it (short)

- **CK3**: men-at-arms regiments with counters, and terrain plus the commander's skill give an "advantage" number before the dice; crossing a river or landing from the sea costs the attacker. Good: the commander and the ground decide the edge before the dice. Taken here: the ground roll. Not taken: counter tables and regiment buying.
- **Imperator**: a tactic chosen per battle that counters another tactic. Close to the battle plate already built; not added to.
- **Total War, Field of Glory**: real battles on a field with many unit stats. Out: no tactical battlefield (war council, all five seats agreed).
- **Republic of Rome**: veteran legions can be loyal to their commander rather than Rome, which is what makes a general dangerous at home. The closest board-game parallel to hearts: an army is a political object.
- **King of Dragon Pass**: a battle is one decision about approach and commitment, told as a short scene with named people who can die or shine. Taken here: few choices, the story of who broke and who held.

## Elephants and later campaigns

- 405 to 395: none. Carthage brought chariots (Ephorus' 400, 14.54.5), and they are the "exotic" arm of this decade.
- The 289 campaign: Pyrrhus' army with elephants (Diodorus 22.8.2, 22.10.2), a foreign king the player can call in or fight. Elephants: +2 on a plain, but a beaten elephant contingent tramples its own side (−1 to its army); light troops cancel their bonus (Panormus, 250, Polybius 1.40, a pattern from after the span).
- Carthage's own elephants: 262 in Sicily (Polybius 1.19), after the game ends in 264. Not in any campaign unless Jacob chooses to break that line.
- Chariots against the 339 Timoleon pattern (river, storm, chariots in confusion) suit a 344 to 338 campaign if one is ever added; none of the six campaigns covers it now.

## Fit with thread 46 (big event scenes)

Agreed with that thread: its battle scene runs ground, plan, clash, pursuit, with a turn where a danger is picked from the state. A wavering or departing contingent is that danger. This design will write, for the scene to read: the ground's plain name and a one-line reason it favours someone; each contingent's display name, side and state at each stage (held, wavered, broke, went over); which contingent made the turning moment and why, as a sentence; and the modifiers each adds.
