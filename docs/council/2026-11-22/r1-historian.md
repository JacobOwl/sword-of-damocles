# Round 1: The Historian

**File key** (all under `/home/user/phoenician-punic-world/sources/`): **D13** `diodorus/english/book13.loeb.txt`; **D14a** `diodorus/english/book14.loeb.txt` (14.41-76, 95-96); **D14b** `diodorus/english/book14-1-18-97-117.loeb.txt`; **D14c** `diodorus/english/book14-78-96.txt`; **DGk** `diodorus/sicilian-wars.greek.txt`; **Just** `justin/justin-18.7-19.1.watson.txt`; **Arist** `governance/aristotle-politics-2.11.txt`; **Hoyos** `modern/hoyos-2006/hoyos-2006.txt`; **Polyaen** `sicily-tyrants/polyaenus-book5.shepherd.txt`. Every quote below I opened and grepped today.

**My stance.** The sources give us a run of *situations* that keep coming back, not a plot to follow. Arming the citizens for war arms the plotters. A general who loses must answer at home. Mercenaries are paid in other people's cities. Allies change sides after a defeat. Build those as rules and the island will make Dionysius-like stories without Dionysius' outcomes. Turn the dates into triggers and we have scripted the history, which the project rules forbid.

## What the sources let the game claim

**The start map is a treaty, and Diodorus gives it to us.** The peace of 405 (D13, 13.114.1): Carthage keeps "together with their original colonists, the Elymi and Sicani"; Selinus, Acragas, Himera, Gela and Camarina "may dwell in their cities, which shall be unfortified, but shall pay tribute"; "Leontini and Messenê and the Siceli shall all live under laws of their own making"; "the Syracusans shall be subject to Dionysius." That is four legal standings (Punic, tributary and unwalled, autonomous, subject), and it is a better starting state than anything we could invent. Gela and Camarina were emptied in 405 (D13, 13.111.3: "compelled the residents of that city also to depart"), so they begin as half-empty places.

**Which cities matter, by what happens in them:**
- *Leontini, Catana, Naxos* (the Chalcidian cities, 403-ish). Taken by treachery or terror, not by storm. Arcesilaus "had offered to betray" Catana, and Procles "delivered over his native city" of Naxos. Naxos was razed, Catana given to the Campanians, and the people of Leontini "removed to Syracuse" (D14b, 14.15.1-4). Leontini first held out because Dionysius had "no engines of war" (D14b, 14.14.4).
- *Messana and Rhegium* (the Straits). Between them they could man "no less than eighty triremes" (D14b, 14.8.2). Rhegium refused him a bride by vote (D14a, 14.44.5) and later gave "asylum to those who were expelled by Dionysius" (D14c, 14.87.1). Himilco razed Messana so thoroughly that "no one would have known that the site had been occupied" (D14a, 14.58.3).
- *Motya*, the Punic base, "most loyal to the Carthaginians", on an island with a causeway (D14a, 14.48.1-2). The cities that stayed loyal to Carthage in 397: "Halicyae, Solûs, Aegesta, Panormus, and Entella" (D14a, 14.48.4).
- *Sikel towns.* Herbessus (D14b, 14.7.5-6); Enna, where Dionysius sponsored a would-be tyrant and then betrayed him (D14b, 14.14.6-8); Assorus, the only Sikel town to stay loyal in 396 (D14a, 14.58.1); Tauromenium, founded by Sikels on Himilco's offer (D14a, 14.59.2); and Agyrium under Agyris, "the strongest armament of any of the tyrants of Sicily at that time after Dionysius", with "no less than twenty thousand citizens" (D14c, 14.95.4).
- *Aetna*, a fortress and a refuge. The rebel cavalry "occupied Aetnê" (D13, 13.113.3), and later "more than seven thousand" exiles gathered there (D14b, 14.9.5).

**How Carthage was governed then: thin, Greek, and partly later.** For 405-392 what we can actually source:
- The war leader was legally made "king". Greek: "Ἰμίλκωνα βασιλέα κατὰ νόμον καταστήσαντες" (DGk, 14.54.5), translated "lawfully according Himilcon sovereign power" (D14a). Hannibal in 409 likewise "lawfully exercised sovereign powers" (D13, 13.43.5).
- There was a senate and a people. Dionysius' letter "was read in the council and subsequently before the people", and Carthage "dispatched members of the senate with large sums of money to recruit mercenaries" (D14a, 14.47.2-3).
- One family dominated. Himilco is "the son of Hanno and of the same family" as Hannibal (D13, 13.80.1). **The sources disagree here:** Justin makes him a son of Hamilcar (Just, 19.2.1). Hoyos calls the Magonids "de facto rulers of Carthage for 150 years, manipulating her republican institutions" (Hoyos, p. 42).
- Generals were held to account. Justin says "a hundred judges were chosen out of the senate, who were to demand of the generals... an account" (Just, 19.2.5-6), but gives no firm date. Himilco "passed sentence of death upon himself and starved himself to death" (D14a, 14.76.4). In Justin he simply "put an end to his life" (Just, 19.3.11).
- *Later, not for 400 BC:* Aristotle's kings, Elders, "Hundred and Four", "Boards of Five" and offices "for sale" (Arist, 1273a). He writes decades later, as an outsider. Suffetes: **unverified** for this period; the saved texts I searched only show them much later (Hoyos on Hannibal as sufete in 196).

**Army sizes are rhetoric with a range attached.** Diodorus himself shows two authorities disagreeing. Ephorus gives Himilco "more than three hundred thousand infantry", while Timaeus says the troops from Libya "did not exceed one hundred thousand", plus 30,000 raised in Sicily (D14a, 14.54.5-6). For 406 the two give "a little over one hundred and twenty thousand" against "three hundred thousand" (D13, 13.80.5). Dionysius' numbers swing just as widely: 80,000 foot at Motya (D14a, 14.47.7), 30,000 at Taurus (D14a, 14.58.2), "not less than twenty thousand" at the Chrysas (D14c, 14.95.3). The plague dead were "one hundred and fifty thousand" (D14a, 14.76.2). Use these as order-of-magnitude texture, never as targets.

**Mercenaries, the Campanians above all, are the island's loose cannon.** They came to Sicily for Athens' war and were rehired by Carthage (D13, 13.44.1-2). Dionysius bought them during the siege of 404 for "any price they should ask", paid them off "having regard to their fickleness", and they then took Entella, where they "slew the men of military age, married the wives" (D14b, 14.8.6, 14.9.8-9). Later they were given Catana (14.15.3) and moved to Aetna (D14a, 14.58.2). The guard's commander, Aristotle the Spartan, was arrested, and the mercenaries, "about ten thousand", took "in lieu of their pay the city and territory of the Leontines" (D14c, 14.78.1-3). Other veterans were settled in Tauromenium (D14c, 14.96.4).

**The war of 398-396 (Diodorus' archon years straddle ours, so give ranges).** Preparation: "one hundred and forty thousand shields" and "more than two hundred ships" (D14a, 14.43.2, 14.42.5). Motya falls to moles and engines; Archylus gets "one hundred minas" for being first on the wall, and Biton is put in charge of the garrison (D14a, 14.53.4-5). Himilco sails under sealed orders (D14a, 14.55.1; also Polyaen 5.10.2). He retakes Motya, razes Messana, and defeats Leptines off Catana, where the Greeks lose "more than one hundred ships and over twenty thousand men" (D14a, 14.60.6). The siege of Syracuse follows, with the temple of Demeter and Kore plundered. Diodorus gives the plague natural causes alongside the divine one: crowding, the season, and "the terrain was marshy and in a hollow" (D14a, 14.70.4-5). Dionysius then lets only the "citizen troops" of Carthage slip away for 300 talents, "in order that the Syracusans... should never find a time of ease to assert their freedom" (D14a, 14.75.1-3). The Libyan revolt after 396 (14.77.1-3) is **not in the saved text**; it needs a paste.

## The nine questions

**Q1. First slice.** *Position:* Start from the 405 treaty map and run 405-395, the same span as Phase 1, so the Citadel cast carries over unchanged. Use eight places: Syracuse, Leontini, Catana, Naxos, Messana, Acragas, Motya, plus Aetna as a fortress, with Rhegium and Carthage off the map. The war of 398 should be possible, not certain. *Proposal:* One loop, with the generalship as the hinge. Every map order needs whoever holds the generalship, and marching means arming citizens. The sources say this outright: once the citizens "had got weapons in their hand, they could strike for their liberty" (D14a, 14.45.5), and the army at Herbessus mutinied "now that they had arms in their hands" (D14b, 14.7.6). *Refuse:* any year-stamped event such as "398: Motya".

**Q2. Carthage.** *Position:* Use a light bot, but one with a constitution: a war general made "king by law", a senate mood, three named men from one family plus a rival house, and the account at home. Do not give Carthage Syracuse's factions; we have no source that supports them. *Proposal:* The mirror. A Carthaginian general's priority list is driven by fear of judgment at home (Just, 19.2.5-6; D14a, 14.76.4), just as the player's is driven by the sword. A beaten general's fall reshapes the senate, and a victorious one grows dangerous to it. *Refuse:* presenting Aristotle's Hundred and Four or suffetes as fact for 400 BC. If used, tag them "invention, from a later description".

**Q3. Armies and war.** *Position:* Armies are tokens with strength, supply and loyalty. Supply decides wars: Dionysius retreated "because the transport of his food supplies was reduced" (D14a, 14.55.5), and Mago was ruined by the Agyrinaeans "continually cutting off the enemy's supplies" (D14c, 14.96.1). Battles are a few choices. Sieges need engines or a traitor. *Proposal:* Each battle choice has a domestic price. "Wait and let want ruin them" is sound, but the citizens may walk off: "Provoked to anger at this the Syracusans deserted him" (D14c, 14.96.2). A victory won without the ruler breeds talk of deposing him; after one, they "talked together about how they took no steps to end their slavery" (D14a, 14.64.3-4). Theodorus' assembly speech is another case (D14a, 14.69.2-4). A defeat abroad makes allies flip: after Tauromenium "the Acragantini and Messenians banished the partisans of Dionysius" (D14c, 14.88.5). Mercenaries carry arrears and can be paid in a city. *Refuse:* tuning army sizes to Diodorus, and battles that kill the player without warning.

**Q4. Other cities.** *Position:* Give each city a few stores (people, walls, grain, ships), its treaty standing, the faction that rules it, and at most one named man where a source names him (Arcesilaus, Procles, Agyris, Aeimnestus). No full faction simulation per city. *Proposal:* Make the treaty standings the relations: subject, tributary and unwalled, autonomous, Punic, or held by mercenaries. Also let cities be emptied and refilled, which the sources show again and again (13.111.3; 14.15.4; 14.58.3; 14.78.5). *Refuse:* fixed borders. The island changed hands by betrayal far more often than by battle.

**Q5. Plots and the sword.** *Position:* Method-and-moment fits the sources best, because the real moments were circumstantial: the army abroad (14.7.6), the ruler away fetching supplies (14.64.1-4), an assembly called in wartime (14.69-70). *Proposal:* Add war moments as plot windows, each announced by a warning. A campaign raises the citizens' strength because they are armed, and lowers the guard's reach. *Refuse:* raising lethality to make war feel dangerous.

**Q6. Rivals.** *Position:* Exile is the missing half of the rival system. Heloris was Dionysius' friend, "or as some say, his adopted father" (D14b, 14.8.5), and later Rhegium's general against him (D14c, 14.87.1). *Proposal:* A rival who flees takes up residence in a named city (Aetna, Rhegium, Carthage), grows there, and comes back with that city's backing. That gives repeat enmity a reason and a place, instead of the same man re-rolling. *Note:* the real Nicoteles was killed early by Aristus (D14b, 14.10.3). A game where he lives and dominates is a fair divergence, but it should not happen in every game.

**Q7. The player's goal.** *Position:* Dynasty and the city's survival are sourced motives. Dionysius married twice at once "in the belief that the loyalty of his offspring would be the strongest safeguard" (D14a, 14.44.5). The other seats should own this one. *Refuse:* a score based on matching history.

**Q8. Voices.** *Position:* The council of advisors is history, not invention. Facing the revolt, Dionysius "gathered together his friends to counsel with them" and heard three opposed opinions (D14b, 14.8.4-5); before Syracuse, "some of his friends" changed his mind (D14a, 14.61.2). *Proposal:* Build the council from the cast, each with a stake, and let the chronicle show disputed counts (see the idea below). *Refuse:* advisors who are neutral explainers.

**Q9. Architecture.** *Position:* History lives in data as *conditions*, not dates. *Proposal:* Plague is a condition: a large army, a marshy camp and summer (14.70.4-5), with sacrilege as a flag on morale. Then it can strike the player's army too. *Refuse:* any check that fails when a run diverges from 397.

## Top three questions
1. **Q3**, war feeding back into politics. It is the one loop the sources document best.
2. **Q2**, Carthage as a constitution with an account at home. It is the mirror, and it is honest about how thin the sources are.
3. **Q4**, cities as treaty standings that can be emptied, betrayed or handed over. The map's grammar rests on this.

## The idea nobody else will raise
**Disputed numbers as a game element.** When an army is reported, the chronicle and the advisors quote *rival historians*: "Ephorus would say three hundred thousand; Timaeus, nearer a hundred." The player's spies narrow the range; the true number is hidden in the state. This is sourced (14.54.5-6, 13.80.5) and costs little. It makes uncertainty something the player feels in play, and it teaches ACOUP-style source criticism without a lecture.

**Unverified or missing:** the Libyan revolt (14.77.1-3) and the dates of the hundred judges and of the suffetes. Gela's and Camarina's standing after 396 I have not checked. The Athenian archon-year to BC conversions are standard but not re-checked here.
