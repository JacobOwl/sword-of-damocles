THE HISTORIAN: Dionysius I's wars and the towns of Sicily

I only grepped the saved texts under /home/user/phoenician-punic-world/sources and opened single sections. Every quote below comes from a section I opened. I checked passage numbers against all 27 cards in /home/user/sword-of-damocles/docs/research/. The ones marked (NEW) are not on any card yet.

**1. Towns' own politics**

- **A defeat triggers a purge.** After Dionysius's failed night attack on Tauromenium: "the Acragantini and Messenians banished the partisans of Dionysius, asserted their freedom, and renounced their alliance with the tyrant" (14.88.5; only on island-events.md as an event). Towns turn on news of a defeat, not because an army is nearby.
  - Hook: each town has a party for you and a party against, each with a named leader. A lost battle or a failed storm anywhere sends news that lets the hostile party move.
- **Leaders who sell the town, and leaders who won't.** Catana and Naxos were betrayed by their own generals (14.15.1-2, already on a card). Phyton of Rhegium is the opposite case (NEW): "he was punished because he would not betray the city to Dionysius" (14.112.3), and Dionysius flogged him around the city because "he had persuaded the city to undertake the war" (14.112.2).
  - Hook: a town leader has a price, or is incorruptible. Breaking an incorruptible one costs you standing, and "some of them began to protest" (14.112.3) among your own soldiers.
- **Split towns.** Messana "could not agree among themselves about the war". One party sent "their children and wives and most valuable possessions to neighbouring cities" (14.56.4). The Carthaginian fleet then took it because the defenders had gone out to block the landing (14.57.1-3, NEW).
  - Hook: a divided town contributes fewer men, its refugees flow to its neighbours, and it falls more easily.
- **A divided town can be set against itself by suspicion.** Polyaenus 5.2.18 (NEW; a late source, 2nd century AD, an anecdote): Dionysius spared "the estates of particular persons" while ravaging, and sent gold to them so they would be tried for treason. "Dissensions arose as a result."
- **Assemblies that vote:** the people of Rhegium refused the marriage "by action of the people" (14.107.3; 14.44.5 already on a card). Acragas's assembly stoned four of its generals for letting the enemy escape (13.87.5, NEW, 406 BC).
  - Hook: a town's assembly can punish its own commanders after a missed chance. That opens its offices.
- **Exiles harboured:** Rhegium "offered asylum to those who were expelled by Dionysius" and then besieged Messana under Heloris (14.87.1, on a card).
  - Hook: a town that shelters your exiles becomes a base for raids against you.
- **Local tyrants:** Agyris ruled 20,000 citizens and kept money he had "collected after he had murdered the wealthiest citizens" (14.95.5). He was bought with a promise of land "if the war ended successfully" (14.95.6). Already partly carded.
- **Settlers placed by Dionysius:**
  - Mercenaries got Leontini "in lieu of their pay" (14.78.2-3, carded).
  - Mercenaries were settled in Tauromenium after "most of the Siceli" were banished (14.96.4).
  - Messana was refilled with Locrians and Medmaeans (14.78.5).
  - Caulonia's people were moved to Syracuse with five years free of tax, and the city levelled (14.106.3, NEW).
  - Polyaenus 5.2.20 (doubtful): a small garrison was made to hold a large town by marrying freed slaves into their masters' families.
- **How towns chose sides: fear of size plus hatred.**
  - "the people of Eryx were awed by the magnitude of the force and, hating the Carthaginians as they did, came over" (14.48.1).
  - Motya resisted because it was "most loyal to the Carthaginians" (same section).
  - The Siceli, "who had hated Dionysius from of old and now had an opportunity to revolt, went over in a body, with the exception of the people of Assorus" (14.58.1).
  - Once Dionysius left, the Halicyaeans "deserted him" (14.55.7).
  - Hook: a town keeps a memory of grievance. The current `lean` number could become a named grievance ("we hate Carthage") plus fear of whoever is strongest nearby.

**2. Carthage's war rhythm (all NEW unless marked)**

- **Wars start when the other side is weak.** Dionysius chose war because "many Carthaginians in Libya had fallen victims to a plague" (14.41.1). Carthage was "totally unprepared" (14.47.2), so it "waited for the Syracusans to take the initiative" and sent senators "with large sums of money to recruit mercenaries" (14.47.3).
  - Hook: Carthage's weakness shows on the board and invites your declaration. Its first act in a war is to hire, not to land.
- **Casus belli (a pretext for war):** in 383 Dionysius took in Carthage's revolting towns. "The Carthaginians at first dispatched ambassadors... and asked for the return of their cities, and when he paid no attention to them, this came to be the beginning of the war" (15.15.1-2).
  - Hook: accepting a defecting Carthaginian town gives Carthage a demand with a deadline. Ignoring it raises Carthage's anger.
- **Recovery is slow and political.** "after a slow recovery from the disaster" (14.95.1). Magon "showed kindness to the subject cities and received the victims of Dionysius' wars" (14.90.3, carded).
- **Terms.** The texts show five kinds:
  - "like the former except that the Siceli were to be subject to Dionysius and that he was to receive Tauromenium" (14.96.4).
  - The victor's maximum demand: "retiring from the cities of Sicily and paying the cost of the war" (15.15.4).
  - The stalling truce: they "pretended that they were satisfied... asked Dionysius to agree to a truce of a few days", and in it the new general "rendered the army obedient and competent" (15.16.1-2).
  - The indemnity and a border on a river: "the Carthaginians received... Selinus... and that of Acragas as far as the river called Halycus. And Dionysius paid the Carthaginians one thousand talents" (15.17.5).
  - A one-year truce with Rhegium after ravaging (14.90.7).
  - Hook: peace becomes a short menu (each keeps what it holds; this town ceded; land to a river; silver paid; a fixed-term truce). A truce can be a trap.
- **The secret bribe** (14.75.1-3, carded): Dionysius took 300 talents and let Carthage's citizens escape "in order that the Syracusans, by reason of their fear of the Carthaginians, should never find a time of ease to assert their freedom."
  - Hook: the player may *want* Carthage alive. A total victory lowers the fear that keeps you in power.

**3. How campaigns worked (NEW unless marked)**

- **Supply decides when to fight.** Dionysius declined battle "because he was widely separated from his allied cities and because the transport of his food supplies was reduced" (14.55.5). He then marched home "laying waste the territory" (14.55.7).
  - Magon was "in hostile territory and was ever more and more in want of supplies", while Agyris's men were "continually cutting off the enemy's supplies" (14.96.1).
  - At Acragas in 406, cavalry "covering the roads... seized such as were foraging" (13.88.1).
  - Hook: an army's food depends on how many days it is from a friendly town that will feed it. A friendly local lord on the route ambushes enemy convoys.
- **Ravaging as a weapon and as pay:** Himilcon "for thirty days overran the countryside, cutting down the trees... not only to satisfy the soldiers with every kind of plunder, but also to reduce the besieged to despair" (14.62.5).
  - Hook: area devastation (Jacob's own idea) feeds your army and lowers a besieged town's will.
- **Refusing battle costs patience.**
  - The Sicilian Greeks left "some of them departing to their own countries and others to fortresses" (14.61.3).
  - "time and want would ruin the barbarians without fighting. Provoked to anger at this the Syracusans deserted him" (14.96.2).
  - Hook: holding off is a strong strategy, but allied and citizen contingents drain away each season you do it.
- **Mercenaries' pay:**
  - Unpaid mercenaries demanded rations and "threatened to go over to the enemy". Himilcar gave the Carthaginians' goblets "as a pledge" (13.88.2-3).
  - Campanians "were bought off for fifteen talents and went over to the Carthaginians" (13.88.5).
  - Devereaux (ACOUP part 2): most mercenary pay "seems to have been due at discharge". That comes from Polybius about the 3rd century, so apply it only by analogy.
  - Hook: arrears pile up and come due at peace. The enemy can buy your hired men.
- **The fleet:**
  - A ten-ship raid on the harbour was meant to "force Dionysius to send part of his fleet back" (14.49.1-2).
  - Fifty transports were caught at sea (14.55.3).
  - Sealed orders hid the landing place (14.55.1).
  - The besieger built provisioned forts and sent "merchant ships to Sardinia and Libya to secure grain" (14.63.3-4).
  - Before an invasion Dionysius went about "strengthening the fortresses and storing them with provisions" (14.58.1).
- **Sieges end by hunger more than by storm.** Rhegium surrendered in "about the eleventh month"; people ate "the grass near the walls" and Dionysius "brought in cattle to clear the place" (14.111.1-3).

**4. Battle (all NEW)**

- **A death on a wing breaks the army.** Leptines fell "on one wing... At his fall the Phoenicians were emboldened". Dionysius's own select troops were winning, "but when the death of Leptines became known and the other wing was crushed, his men were dismayed and took to flight" (15.17.1-2).
  - At Cabala, Magon died and the Carthaginians "at once sent an embassy to discuss terms" (15.15.3-4).
  - Hook: a battle has two wings and a centre, each led by a named person. A commander's death can turn a winning side into a rout and can open peace talks.
- **The camp and nightfall:** "The survivors, who found safety in the camp, were preserved by the coming of night" (15.17.4). After Cabala the losers were trapped "on a hill which was fortified but altogether without water" (15.15.3).
  - Hook: the loser chooses between a camp (safe if one is near) and a refuge with its own risk.
- **Rashness:** Leptines went "far ahead of the rest... in no cowardly fashion, but without prudence" (14.60.2), and "the defeat suffered by the admiral... markedly discouraged the Sicilian Greeks" (14.60.4).
  - Hook: a general's traits can become a stance (bold or careful) rather than a flat bonus.
- **A surprise attack on a sick camp** by land and sea together (14.72.1-4). Dionysius ordered his cavalry to flee so the mercenaries "most hostile... to Dionysius" died "to a man" (14.72.3).
- **Winning without the tyrant is a political danger.** The Syracusans' sea victory without Dionysius led to talk of how to "end their slavery" (14.64.3-4). Theodorus: "when he is absent, even a small detachment is sufficient" (14.69.3).
  - Hook: a victory by a rival commander raises that rival's standing against you.
- **Leading in person is risky:** at Tauromenium Dionysius "barely escaped being taken alive" (14.88.3).

**Where the texts are silent or weak**

- No grain amounts, pay rates or march speeds in Diodorus for this period.
- The army numbers are inflated and disputed: 300,000 according to Ephorus against 100,000 plus 30,000 according to Timaeus (14.54.5-6). Don't use them as sizes.
- 14.77.1-3 (the Libyan revolt) is saved in Greek only; I could not quote it in English.
- The dates and sites of Cabala and Cronium are uncertain (Devereaux: "383 to 381 (?)"), and they fall outside the 405-395 window. Use them as patterns, not as Phase 2 events.
- Polyaenus (5.2.9, 5.2.10, 5.2.18, 5.2.20, 5.10.5) is late and anecdotal; label any use of it "doubtful".
- Diodorus gives almost no tactics beyond wings, rout and camp. ACOUP part 5's tactics (double envelopment, cavalry) are drawn from Polybius on the Punic Wars.
- The texts say nothing about how a Carthage-held town governed itself.

**Top five, ranked**

1. **Town parties with named leaders, and news as the trigger** (medium). Defeats anywhere let the hostile party purge and flip the town (14.88.5). Leaders can be bought (14.15) or refuse (14.112.3). This replaces the single `lean` roll and the 1-silver embassy flip.
2. **Supply by distance to a feeding town, with refusing battle drawing on a patience clock** (medium). Sources: 14.55.5, 14.95.7-14.96.2, 14.61.3. It makes holding off a real choice, and it is how wars actually ended.
3. **Peace with content** (medium). A defecting town gives Carthage a pretext, then a demand; terms are ceding a town, a river line, an indemnity, or a fixed-term truce that can be a trap. Add the "keep Carthage alive" temptation (14.75.3, 15.15-17).
4. **Pay arrears and land-for-pay settlers** (cheap to medium). Unpaid men demand pay or defect (13.88); paying in a town's land plants a garrison that changes that town (14.78.2, 14.96.4). The "Pay in land" event already exists.
5. **A battle with two wings and a centre under named commanders, plus a camp** (medium). A death breaks the line or opens talks (15.17.1-2, 15.15.4). A rival's victory without you hurts you (14.64.3).

**What NOT to do:** don't make pitched battle the main engine of the war, and don't import Hannibal-era tactics or Diodorus's troop numbers. In these texts wars are decided by hunger, plague, desertion, betrayal and deals far more often than by the battle roll.
