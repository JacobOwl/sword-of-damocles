## 1. Invading Libya

### What Carthage's home country held

- **Rich farms and villas.** Agathocles' men saw "gardens and plantations of every kind", "country houses one after another ... covered with stucco", vines, olives, "herds of cattle and flocks of sheep" (Diodorus 20.8.3 to 20.8.4, `diodorus/english/book20.loeb.txt`). Polybius on Regulus' landing: "numerous dwelling houses of remarkably fine construction", cattle, and "more than twenty thousand slaves" (1.29, `polybius/book1.shuckburgh.txt`).
- **Many open towns.** Agathocles gained "all the cities, which were more than two hundred in number" (20.17.6). Justin says they "were not secured with walls ... but lay in level plains" (22.5.5, `justin/book22-23.watson.txt`). The two sources disagree in tone: Diodorus has several sieges (Utica, Hippu Acra, 20.55.1 to 20.55.3).
- **Four peoples.** "the Phoenicians, who at that time occupied Carthage; the Libyphoenicians, who have many cities along the sea"; the Libyans, "most numerous and oldest", who "hated the Carthaginians with a special bitterness"; and "the Nomads" (20.55.4).
- **A heavy hand.** In the First Punic War Carthage "exacted half of all agricultural produce; had doubled the tribute of the towns" (Polybius 1.72). That is 264 to 241, not 398. Do not copy the rate back.

### How Carthage raised armies from Libya

- 410: Hannibal "visited Libya, choosing the stoutest men from every city" (13.44.6, `book13.loeb.txt`).
- 406: the generals "canvassed Libya, enrolling as soldiers Libyans and Phoenicians and the stoutest from among their own citizens", plus allies "Maurusians and Nomads" (13.80.3).
- Devereaux reads this as conscription, not hire: the Libyans were "paid conscripts, serving ... long terms, their recruitment presumably part of the deal Carthage imposed on subject North African communities". Most pay "seems to have been due at discharge" (`acoup/raising-carthaginian-armies-2.devereaux.md`). He says older scholars thought they were mercenaries, and that Polybius' word misthophoroi misleads. This is a live disagreement; show both.

**Hook, invention:** Carthage's Libyan bloc already exists in `data/carthage.json`. Its anger could rise with each levy and with soldiers who do not come home paid. That follows Devereaux and 14.77.1.

### What fits 398 to 395

No Syracusan army went to Libya in these years. I found nothing in the saved texts that says otherwise. What did happen:

- **A herald to Carthage, 398/397.** Dionysius "sent a herald to Carthage, having given him a letter to the senate", saying Syracuse would make war "unless they withdrew from the Greek cities". It "had been read in the council and subsequently before the people" (14.47.1 to 14.47.2, `book14.loeb.txt`).
- **Grain from Carthage's own waters, 396.** "He also dispatched merchant ships to Sardinia and Libya to secure grain" (14.63.4). Whose ports in Libya is not said.
- **A tyrant who did not want Carthage broken, 396.** He let the citizen troops go "in order that the Syracusans, by reason of their fear of the Carthaginians, should never find a time of ease to assert their freedom" (14.75.3). Himilcon paid "the three hundred talents" (14.75.4).
- **The Libyan rising after 396.** Only 14.77.1 in Greek (gloss above). Then plague and fear: "the entire city was seized by superstitious fear" (14.77.4).

### What belongs to later campaigns

- **Agathocles, 310 to 307.** The second front: "cross over into Libya" (20.3.3); his brother "curator of the city" (20.4.1); the vow to burn the ships, "in victory alone would they have hope of safety" (20.7.5); towns come over "because of fear, others because of their hatred" (20.17.1); the Libyan king "Aelymas ... who had deserted him" (20.18.3). His reasoning in Justin: "they would not continue to besiege Syracuse, when they were suffering from a siege of their own city" (22.5.10).
- **Carthage's answer to an invader.** Three armies, so that "the allies would continue more loyal if there were more armies in the field" (20.59.1 to 20.59.3). Refugees in the city cause "a general scarcity" (20.59.2). At the end, captives "by their own labour" restore "the country they had laid waste" (20.69.5).
- **An African ally.** Agathocles invites Ophellas of Cyrene, promising "dominion over Libya" (20.40.2, `book20-gaps.txt`), then kills him (20.42.3 to 20.42.5).
- **Regulus, 256 to 255.** Takes Aspis, plunders, then Numidians do "even more damage to the country than the Romans" and the country folk "flock for safety into the city" (Polybius 1.31). His terms were "so severe" that they refused (1.31). The hired Spartan Xanthippus turned the war (1.32).

### Options

- **A. In 398 to 395, Libya is a place you send to, not march in.** A herald, grain buyers, and the Libyan bloc's anger inside Carthage's council. Source: 14.47, 14.63.4, 14.77.1.
- **B. A labelled what-if raid on Libya** (invention, Agathocles as template, off by default).
- **C. The full second front** in the Agathocles campaign, as `full-game.md` already plans.

I pick A now and C later. B has no precedent before 310 in the saved texts. If Jacob wants it, it must say "invention" on screen.

**Not saved, unverified:** Athens and Dionysius (inscriptions of 393 and 368); Xenophon on ships to Sparta (387, 369/368); Lysias' *Olympiacus* itself; any Syracuse and Persia contact. Persia appears in 14.79 to 14.80 only as Sparta's war in Asia in 396. Do not build a Persian or Athenian deal for 398 to 395.

**Contact with Carthage's enemies in Africa before Agathocles:** none found in the saved texts. Nearest things: grain from "Sardinia and Libya" (14.63.4); workmen hired from "Carthaginian territory" (14.41.3); Carthaginians living rich in Syracuse, plundered in 398 (14.46.1); Dion's "letter ... to the Carthaginian officials", about 366 (Plutarch *Dion* 14, `plutarch/dion.perrin.txt`), which is contact with Carthage, not its enemies; and "six hundred Africans" sent by a Dionysius (Justin 23.1.11; which Dionysius is unclear). Nothing says Dionysius stirred the Libyan rising of 396.

**Hooks the history suggests (hook, invention):**
- An embassy is a person sent and a reply that comes seasons later (14.62.1 to 14.63.4).
- A marriage offer is put to the other city's assembly and can be refused (14.44.5 to 14.44.6). The refusal is remembered: Diodorus says the siege of Rhegium came "because of the slight he had received in connection with his offer of marriage" (14.106.1).
- Spartan help comes with Spartan aims. It props up the ruler, not the city (14.10.2, 14.70.2). Good for you as tyrant, bad for your standing with the people.
- An ally city's generals can march without a vote, and the army can go home (14.40.4 to 14.40.5).

## 3. How off-map powers should appear

The sources show these powers through people who travel: a herald, a brother-in-law, an admiral. Near ones are physically close: Rhegium and Messana face each other across the Strait (14.44.3).

- **A. Strait cities on the map, the rest as a list of envoys with travel time.**
- **B. Everything as a list.**
- **C. A second map for Italy.**

I pick A. Locri, Rhegium and Messana are part of the 398 story. Sparta and Corinth act through envoys and ships that arrive later. C belongs with the Italian wars after 393.

## 4. What not to do

- Do not let Carthage fall in 398 to 395. No source has it near falling then. Its home was hit by plague and revolt, not by Greeks (14.77.1, 14.77.4).
- Do not make Sparta simply a friend. It helped the tyrant and betrayed the city (14.70.3: "Aretes ... had betrayed them").
- Do not call the Libyan soldiers plain mercenaries. Show the disagreement (Devereaux versus the older reading).
- Do not move later events into 398 to 395: the Italian league, the Lucanians, the Adriatic, Pyrgi, Regulus, Hieron.
- Do not apply Polybius' "half of all agricultural produce" to the 390s.
- Do not use Persia or Athens until a source is saved.
